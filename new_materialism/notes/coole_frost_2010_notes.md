### Recite: Consider what you want to remember, and put that into your own words
There is a lot to unpack in this chapter, for sure. Coole and Frost offer a comprehensive tracing of the underlying themes that shape new materialist scholarship, as well as trace the philosophical genealogies that this scholarship both critiques, builds on, and responds to. For example, they opened by first explaining the ontological Debate that has pervaded various philosophical schools of thought particularly since Descartes. They explain that Descartes' famous _cogito ergo sum_ points to not only an ontological distinction between the human subject and the objective material world, but that there are various sociopolitical implications that emerge from this ontological perspective. This idea reminded me quite a bit of James Scott's (1998) description of how states develop measurement tools in order to map out the world around them in a way that is aligned with Cartesian rationality. In this case, objects and matter are not only ontologically distinct from humans and human subjects, but they can also be categorized, counted, mapped, visualized, and measured in a way that suggests that humans somehow ontologically have a right to dominate the objective world around them. The political implications for such an ontological perspective are clear, especially as highlighted by Sylvia Wynter (2003), where she explains how Western, white, European, Cartesian logics and emphasis on rationality have been weaponized in terms of which populations are permitted by dominant, oppressive forces to be read as fully subjects. In other words, Wynter argues that an overemphasis on the role and power of the human carries political risk in that this power has never been distributed equitably, particularly as is the case for marginalized communities in the Global South, and Black and brown communities across the world. Overall, what stands out to me about Coole and Frost's argument here is that they are not only stating that new materialism is pushing toward a monist framework as opposed to a dualist or cartesian one, but that to do so is itself a particularly political act due to the implications that an ontological perspective has in shaping social practices, policies, behaviors, norms, ideologies, and more.

They then shift to discuss another theme that is a central focus in new materialist scholarship, particularly in a way that illustrates the political and ethical implications of the ontological perspectives that scholars take in a very applied way. Here, they look at new materialist scholars investment in critical interrogation of issues of bio power, biotechnologies, and bioethics to consider how biological materials or matter that has been previously ontologically distinguished from human life is instead actively present in a much larger end entangled system. They point to specific examples of how a new materialist perspective could benefit exploring biological problems, such as ethical issues relating to the development of gene technologies that could potentially enable greater human control over the production of life, or ethical questions raised when looking at the larger systematic effects of climate change and how to contend with individual action that plays a role in that system, or even the risks posed by greater human control over biological technology which could unintentionally or intentionally lead to consequences such as the development of new bioterror weapons. 

They finally closeout their discussion of these three key themes that are shaping new materialist scholarship to consider the political economy of materials and matter, especially through the development of thinking of scholars such as Foucault, Marx, and Althusser. They essentially point to some of the limitations of Marxist theory, namely its tendency toward a more teleological or idealist understanding of the history of capitalism and how it unfolds on a very macro scale. These limitations they feel are addressed more readily through New Materialist perspectives that give more attention to corporeal experience and the phenomenological experience of people, which helps to address some of the gaps that emerge from a solely macro perspective to sociological and materialist analysis. At the same time, cool and frost to make it explicitly clear that new materialism does not holy rejection of Marxist thought or macro analysis, Instead they argue that it is incredibly important to also account for complex relations between the state, institutions, social structures, and individual experience, and these relationships should specifically be investigated with attention to the role of materials and matter in shaping and being shaped by those system.

### Relate: Identify connections or links to other readings and scholarship
Just as this chapter is largely organized by Coole and Frost's examination of the three themes that they feel undergird much of contemporary new materialist scholarship, I found that the connections between this piece to scholarship that I have encountered also tended to follow along that theme structure. For example, I saw a lot of connections between Coole and Frost's explanation of the ontological limitations of rationalist, Cartesian thinking with Manuel De Landa's (1997) proposal for a historiography of the material world that takes a more dynamic, nonlinear ontological perspective. There were even some mentions of issues of bifurcation that came up in relation to chaos theory and complexity theory, which stood out to me because of Thomas Nail's (2019) interest in bifurcation as a way of theorizing the circulation and flow of materials in response to a border. Of course, there are very clear alignments here with much of Deleuze and Guattari's work particularly as discussed in _A Thousand Plateau's_, which again points to yet another connection to De Landa's work. Furthermore, in terms of issues of bioethics and bio politics, I could see some connections here to Timothy Mitchell's (2002) work especially around the roll of the mosquito as an agent in shaping global politics and relations during the 19th and early to mid 20th centuries. Finally, much of the discussion at the end of the chapter that pertained to critical materialist analysis clearly picks up on long line of economic and sociological theory, including the work of Pierre Bourdieu, Michel Foucault, Karl Marx, Louis Althusser, Max Weber, Henri Lefebvre, and Fernand Braudel.

## Intervention, Argument, Evidence
### Intervention: What is the intervention that the scholar is making, and where is that intervention being made? In other words, how do you place that intervention relative to existing disciplinary conversations that are taking place?
In this case, it seems that Cool and Frost are laying out a trajectory for how new material a scholarship has emerged, and outline several of the interventions that that scholarship seeks to make, particularly in critiquing and responding to the limitations of a Cartesian dualist perspectives , highlighting the importance of a more robust theorizing about matter and materiality, and ensuring that that is done in a way that is attentive to the political and ethical implications of matter.

### Argument: What are the core argument(s) being made in the text?
Their argument seems to be largely centered around that three part thematic schema that Coole and Frost present. First they explain how new materialism is ontologically distinct from classical ontologies that have dominated Western philosophy up to this point. Then they argue that such a shift in ontological theorizing necessitates greater consideration of the ethical problems and risks that come theorizing about human life in a new way that requires that we interrogate our prior understanding of biological life and our role in the biological world . Finally, They outline the relationship between new materialist thinking and earlier material perspectives from thinkers such as Marx, Althusser, and Freud.

### Evidence: What evidence is used to support the argument(s)?
In regards to the first theme that they cover, Coole and Frost outline changes in scientific understandings of matter in the material world, especially as evident in the shift from Newtonian or classical models of physics to quantum mechanics. In their discussion of bioethics and bio politics, they discuss issues such as the development and use of genetically modified organisms, the ethical implications for assisted suicide, and questions about humans increasing ability through medical and technological advancement to extend their own lives or modify their genetics and biology. The final section relies not so much on examples or evidence to support their argument so much as they describe central issues that have underlied sociological theory and economic theory since the 19th century, evaluating these issues in relationship to the political ethos and ontological perspective that they explain in the first two portions of the chapter.

### How: What methods and theories are used to make the intervention or argument and support it?
It seems like Coole and Frost are mostly focused on offering a literature review or genealogy of new materialist scholarship to frame the rest of the chapters for this edited collection's introduction.
