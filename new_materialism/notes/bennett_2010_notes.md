#### Chapter One
Bennett opens her book by explicitly outlining her interest in a vital materialism as one that is ethically and politically motivated in nature, particularly as she feels that recognizing the active, affective nature of material or "things" offers possibility in addressing various socio political, environmental, and cultural issues through decentering of anthropocentric perspectives on those issues. She specifically identifies herself as contributing to a body of vitalist scholarship including work from Democritus, Epicurus, Spinoza, Diderot, and Deleuze. Though her work is ultimately monist in nature, positioning humans ontologically on the same plane as all other things, she states that she is not solely interested in disproving dualistic ontological perspectives, instead considering and responding to various philosophical projects and perspectives that have sought to understand the tense relationship between subject and object, or human subjectivity in relationship to the material world. 

She introduces the concept of _thing-power_ as an affirmation of the self-sustaining inclination of things and matter, as an extension of Spinoza's _conatus_ or Nietzche's _will to power._ As an example, she describes how her attention was drawn a pile of debris that she encounters on the street in Baltimore near the Chesapeake Bay. She explains how seeing a discarded cap and glove, A rat that may very well be dead , a piece of wood and a mat of oak pollen all provoke a response from her. She explains that while she could reflect on some of the implications of these things, whether it has to be with environmental issues and discarding of litter and the like, But what especially interests her is that the vitality of these objects it's such that she's made aware that they can never be fully captured simply through their representation:

"In this assemblage, _objects_ appeared as _things_, that is, as vivid entities not entirely reducible to the context in which (human) subjects set them, never entirely exhusted by their semiotics" (p.5).

She likens this sense of knowing that there is something unknowable about the material world to Adorno's negative dialectics, or his method for more closely interrogating and accepting these object's non-identity, or its aspects that are not representable or that escape our ability to fully understand, a stark departure from a Hegelian dialectics that pertually seeks to attain the entire truth. Ultimately, without necessarily wholly rejecting the political import of constructivism, Bennett explains that vitalist materialism itself offers another lens for encountering the world through a rejection of anthropocentrism.

#### Chapter Two
Bennett largely focuses on introducing the concept of an assemblage in this chapter, describing them as collection, configuration, or coming together of bodies that, as a result of their collectivity begins to take more capabilities on the basis of how each of the bodies within an assemblage are able to be affected and affect others. It is from this coming together of bodies that are agentic and affective in nature that agency is distributed across the assemblage, rather than located within the sum of any of its constituent parts. She discusses the North American blackout of 2003 as a case in point , considering how all of the actants present in that assemblage, whether they be humans, power plants, electrical wires, electrons, or fire, come to have far reaching consequences that ultimately affected more than 50 million residents as a result of the wide reaching distribution of agentic power that these individual bodies only come to enact through their aggregation or connection to this larger network.
