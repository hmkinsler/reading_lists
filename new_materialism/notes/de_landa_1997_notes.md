### Survey: Skim the text and identify main ideas
De Landa's book follows a three-part structure, tracing history from 1000 - 2000 CE from the lens of geological, biological, and linguistic flows. Given this structure and the intentional progression that he draws on from chapter to chapter to outline his major points, I likewise have organized this memo based on his three-part structure, as well as the introduction that outlines the basis for this organization and his overall reflections on viewing history from the lens of different kinds of materials. In each section, I synthesize key points from his chapters, drawing connections to my own research and the scholarship I am currently engaging both in this class and beyond. Though very accessible in its presentation, as a theoretical text that draws heavily from the work of Deleuze and Guattari, the primary aim of this memo is to understand and reflect on the possible uses of object oriented ontologies in tracing historical processes and the various actors involved in those processes.

#### Introduction
- Explains how historians like Braudel have shaped philosophical thought on history by way of their materialist analyses -- he is basically using the introduction to make an argument about the intertwining of history and philosophy and the necessity of viewing the historical processes from which a number of things emerge -- an ontology of history
    
- The role of non-linear approaches to social sciences, and history, in particular, as history (as a process) and its contingencies are rendered invisible in instances where linear causality is given primacy
    
- Argues for a “bottom-up” approach to history, one that looks at historical development on various scales in such a way that is neither solely focused on individual subjects or their collective societies (with the latter often being a critique of various strains of Marxist analysis and thought)
    
- Locating the historical development of Western societies and their domination of oppressed societies in terms of issues of material accumulation in the context of power and social forces and relations, as opposed to the limited sense of “progress” associated with Western or Eurocentric views of modernity, development, etc.
    
- Looking at history as a process of *accumulation* -- his chapters look at accumulation of various kinds:
    
    - Geologic
        
    - Biologic
        
    - Linguistic
        
- Mentions the issue of bifurcation -- things branching off into two parts
    
    - Nail (2016) *Theory of the Border* -- offers a compelling figure on the issue of bifurcation and historical solidification in urban design and the construction of various material structures (i.e. border walls, cells, etc.) in Fig I.1 on page four of the intro

#### I: LAVAS AND MAGMAS
De Landa begins his three-part investigation into multiple material histories by first tracing the circulation of geological materials in history, with a particular focus on what he describes as processes of mineralization, calcification, or other geological accumulations of various kinds. He highlights the accumulation of calcium and other minerals that give rise to the bones and skeletons of early humans, for example, as part of a historical process that thus gives way to new forms of movement that were otherwise not accessible to pre-skeletal life. He extends this type of solidifying process as akin to the accumulation of geological and mineral materials used to build structures in towns and cities, such as walls, homes, and roads. Here, the accumulation of these materials in one place again makes new kinds of movement possible, as local communities are newly able to organize themselves and their lives around a central place, as opposed to moving in the more migratory patterns that they had previously in order to find adequate food and materials.

This interest in the accumulation of materials in cities, in particular, mirrors some of the disciplinary interests of the historians he cites notably including Fernand Braudel, whose research has sought to understand the historical processes by which cities developed and began to connect with each other through the development of trade routes and the emergence of a global economic network. Here, he begins to outline the role of geological materials in terms of the emergence of nations, which both contain central places (capitals) and gateways (as connections) in a larger entangled network where these city structures are situated in relation to one another. Here, he extends the central place and gateway city model to argue that central places are hierarchical, homogeneous, and thus stratified, drawing on the particular process by which a place becomes organized in a distinctly layered way as described by Deleuze and Guattari (1987). Gateway cities, in contrast, are heterogeneous, more akin to a meshwork or self contained aggregate, as they act as the meeting point between central places in the flow of goods, people, and other resources or materials. He thus ends the chapter by arguing that following the flow and circulation of geological materials in urban development, and thus exploring the contingencies of such flows as any given city takes the form of either a gateway or a central place, illustrates the necessity of nonlinear approaches to history as these models require a certain level of intensity for the flows of these materials to reach the point of accumulation and also require for flows to reflect back on themselves, either to strengthen or impede and thus redirect them.

##### **Geological History: 1700-2000 A.D.**
- Conurbations -- “extremely dense but weakly centralized urban areas produced by accelerated industrialization”
    
- Regions with gateways and centrals, but with industrialization and the harnessing of coal, new cities that are unregulated begin to emerge quickly and diversely
    
- Failures of industrialization to make way for self-sustaining systems before 1800 due to lack of complexity
    
    - Highlights Braudel’s discussion of Milan, Lübeck, and Cologne as three examples
        
    - Also England, writing “one possible explanation is that autocatalytic loops need to achieve a threshold of complexity before they acquire the resilience and versatility needed to overcome diminishing returns” (p. 78)
        
- Shifts to cities with self-sustained industrial takeoffs occurs after 1800, due to “new intensification in agriculture” (p. 78) and the development of skilled knowledges that workers employ to address out-of-balance catalytic loops that lead to self-destruction of the larger system
    
    - “…not only is a diagram necessary (brought into the process by an engineer) but also the skilled manual knowledge needed to implement the abstract diagram” (p. 79)
        
- Meshworks as networks
    
    - “…the process of industrial takeoff may be viewed as a bifurcation, from a state in which self-stimulating dynamics were not complex enough to overcome diminishing returns, to a state in which the series of nodes forming the circuit became a self-sustaining entity. The addition of new nodes to the meshwork as it complexified did not occur according to a plan but simply following internal constraints; that is, each new node had to ‘mesh well’ with the existing ones (i.e. catalyze and be catalyzed by existing nodes)” (pp. 80-81).
        
- Industrialization in the US and the militant overseeing of the railroad (and the railroad anti-market) leads to the rationalization of labor such that skilled workers no longer maintain complex systems and thus enact their control over the system, instead their labor is simplified and thus divided more strictly and solidified by routines so that they are instead controlled by the system, a dynamic that thus brings up issues of surveillance, control, and the imposition of rational norms that are imbued with a linear sense of worker optimality
    
- *Economies of agglomeration* (p. 85) and the generation of knowledge
    
    - G.E. lab is one example De Landa offers
        
- Increasing institutional hierarchies (i.e. greater reliance on middle management and managerial control)
    
- The intensification of energy as manifested by the development of the motor
    
- “Old” and “new” or emergent technologies merging in the meshwork
    
    - “The original loop (coal-iron-steam-cotton), and its newly acquired nodes (railroads, telegraph), continued to function into the twenties century. The new technologies simply grafted themselves into the previous meshwork, become yet other nodes, participating in its self-production and, hence, reproducing themselves” (p. 92)
        
- The rise of technical and scientific research and education
    
    - Committees and *technostructures* (Galbraith, 1967, pp. 66-67)
        
    - The development of Silicon Valley, perhaps a connection to the emergence of PARC and the myth of ubiquitous computing, or UBICOMP (Dourish & Bell 2011)
        
    - Computers’ enmeshment: “Far from having brought society to a new stage of its development, the information stage, computers have simply intensified the flow of knowledge, a flow which, like any other catalyst, still needs matter and flows to be effective” (p. 98)
        
- Transnational corporations’ tension with nation-states in terms of anti-markets and systems of control
    
- (Universal) history as contingent

##### **Biological History: 1000-1700 A.D.**
- Looking at ecosystems: "…the emergence of an ecosystem is typically described as a *succession of plant assemblages*" (p. 105)
    
- Explores relation between medieval cities and their ecosystems (i.e. biological, ecological, environmental, etc.)
    
- Human control over biomass energy flows (i.e. in terms of production/consumption of food): "Together, humans and their 'extended family' of domesticates, as the historian Alfred Crosby calls it, transformed a heterogeneous meshwork of species (i.e. a temperate forest) into a homogeneous hierarchy, since all biomass now flowed toward a single point at the top" (p. 108)
    
- Talks about issues of dynamic and stable systems in terms of germs or pathogens, particular with issues of epidemics and immunity arising out of endemics in medieval cities -- a phenomenon De Landa links to population density and the aggregation of people into a particular place
    
- Disease prevention as a cultural practice that is entangled in this pathogenic meshwork: "These were, in a sense, cultural materials that accumulated unconsciously, sorted out by the pressure of the parasites themselves. Hence, germs and humans formed a meshwork, coevolving through drift, in start contrast with the rest of the food hierarchy at the service of urban culture" (p. 110)
    
- Competition between culture (i.e. humans seeking to exert power over the system) and famines/epidemics
    
- The historically contingent nature of evolution
    
- Genes as coding and encoded (p. 112)
    
- Social classes as being organized into social niches-- having limits placed on their role or place in the urban ecosystem and the types of biomass or energy they have access too (i.e. bread versus imported goods)
    
- Much of his argument in this chapter is focused on this tension between sociocultural control over ecosystems and other factors that intrude: "[Issues of soil erosion and cultivation are] another example of the practical limits of bounded rationality, and proof that, although some material and energy flows can be 'socialized' (i.e. submitted to cultural control), *in practice* many are not" (p. 122)
    
- Cities' ecologies as determining the degree to which they rely on trade, particular in terms of how gateway (or "network cities", p. 127) and central place cities are situated in relationship to the land and cities around them
    
- De Landa links these gateway cities to the emergence of antimarkets, particularly because their lack of intrinsic food production and the like lead them to be reliant on trade networks, and in order to sustain themselves, it makes sense that control over capital (as is the case for antimarkets) would need to be enacted: "In summary, according to Braudel, the European economy comprised three spheres or layers: the interial peasant layer, which was the source of biomass flow; the market economy, which set surpluses into motion by means of the flow of money; and the antimarket, where money detached itself from biomass, becoming a mobile mutant flow capable of investing in any activity that intensified the production of profits" (p. 129)
    
- Population accumulation or aggregation, diseases becoming endemic to a population, and what this means for colonization: "Cultural materials flowed together with genes and biomass (not all of it human) across the Atlantic, and it was the whole complex mixture that triumphed" (p. 133)

##### **Species and Ecosystems**
- Offers some commentary on meshworks vs. rhizomes (p. 138) that is worth responding to in favor of recognizing Deleuze and Guattari’s discussion on *matter* as the BwO or plane of immanence
    
- Distinguishes biological organizing processes in terms of being able to organize entire *populations* in a way that was not possible for geological organizing processes (p. 139)
    
- Focuses on evolutionary processes
    
- Traces the emergence of social Darwinism, its countering with the emergence of cultural relativism, and shifts to an interactionist model
    
    - Interactionist views --> “One version of this new approach (the one developed by William Durham) seems particularly close to the view we are exploring here: that both organic and cultural change involve replicators and that new structures arise by selective retention of variants” (p. 141)
        
- Interactions between genetic/cultural sorting materials as evidenced by patterns in color perception and variation in color words (p. 142)

##### **Biological History: 1700-2000 A.D.**
- Looks at multiple processes of colonization with the influx of European settlers in the Americas, including in terms of the introduction of invasive plants and weeds which are suited for the newly introduced livestock and agricultural practices imposed on this environment by settlers
    
- Uses the term “neo-Europes” to describe the extent of this practice and the idea of a particularly European world being spread and enforced in a variety of ways, often with severe and occasionally unintentional consequences (i.e. the rapid overpopulation of feral cattle once they are introduced in Argentina)
    
- Suppression and repression of other cultural groups in terms of violent colonial practices and the institution of slavery are used to both accumulate resources for European settlers and their colonial powers as well as prevent the possibility of contingent environmental/ecological evolution that lies outside Western, white, European logics
    
- The last two chapters serve as the grounds for understanding this process in which colonization serves to produce “ecological replicas of Europe” (p. 155)
    
- Institutional shifts as a result of biological processes: “Military, medical, educational, and judicial institutions became, in a very real sense, much more “biological” than before: their hierarchies now relied less on tradition and symbolic gestures and began to exercise power in a form increasingly tailored to the functioning of the human body” (pp. 157-158).
    
    - Talks about hospitals as a site for a shift to a focus on biological study and medicine (p. 158)
        
    - Pathologizing of people and institutional enforcement of biocultural and biosocial norms
        
    - Foucault on (1) spatial segmentation (2) inspection and regulatory oversight (3) registration
        
        - Makes me think about “ugly laws” in the US and how they were weaponized against disabled people
            
    - Increased disciplining of the body and its constituent parts (i.e. in movement of soldiers, in agricultural practices and genetic modification for the sake of capitalist production, pedigree breeding)

##### **Linguistic History: 1000-1700 A.D.**
- Looks at historical linguistics and fundamentals of linguistic structures (i.e. morphology, syntax, and phonology) over time, particularly in conversation with language transmission and sociolinguistic parameters that link language variation to social status and groups
    
- Isolation as a factor in linguistic stratification
    
- Dialect contact as a linguistic meshwork or form of heterogeneity
    
- Homogeneity of “standardized” linguistic strata
    
- Emergence of social awareness of linguistic variation thus leading to assignment of social meaning to linguistics forms and subsequent attempts to formalize control over language (i.e. Carolingian reforms as an attempt to standardize Latin) (p. 188)
    
- Language as deixis and indexical (p. 190) -- I like the way Wortham & Reyes (2020) break this down in their methods chapter
    
- “Hence, several social factors come into play in explaining how labels ‘stick’ to their referents: the history of the accumulated uses of a word, the role of experts in determining its reference, and the obligatory acquisition of certain information which counts as part of our ability to use the word” (pp. 190-91)
    
- Looks at social network as a factor of linguistic diversity, change, and spread -- I’m overall really pleased and impressed with this discussion especially given the text’s publication year (1997) and his well-integrated use of sociolinguistic theories on language variation; this chapter/section feels most impressive because of its attention to the disciplinary conversations taking place in that field
    
- Social networks & urban development
    
    - High-density or “closed” networks vs. low-density or “open” networks
        
    - “Given a network of a certain density, the higher the local prestige of an individual, or the larger the number of his or her contacts, the more likely it is that a variant originated by that individual will become collective and eventually become part of the accumulated heritage” (p. 193)
        
        - Connects well to issues of social, economic, and cultural capital (Bourdieu, 1981) and their effects on social network membership
            
- Horizontal linguistic flows (i.e. through language contact between dialect-dialect) and vertical linguistic flows in which norms are passed from generation to generation over time
    
- The linguistic effects of colonization and linguistic essentialism rooted in racism (i.e. linguists who argue that English’s shift over time from a predominantly synthetic language to an analytic one is evidence of some inherent rationality and modern development rather than a matter of a shift from one neutral and equal grammatical system to another)
    
- Looks at major and minor theories (Deleuze and Guattari, 1972, p. 102) in terms of language and dialects
    
- Draws on Austin (1975) to look at speech acts as part of institutional control both through *and* over language: “This simply emphasized that we are not dealing here with a purely linguistic process but with a complex situation involving hierarchies, chains of command, and the means to enforce obedience” (p. 202)
    
- Argues that language variation is a matter of *contingency* that should be explored rather than assuming the basis of language as naturally homogenous
    
    - Talks about the great vowel shift -- does not explicitly touch on changes from above vs. changes from below (surprising given how frequently he draws on Labov’s work) but he is alluding to it here
        
    - “You will never find a homogeneous system that is not already affected by a regulated, continuous, immanent process of variation (why does Chomsky pretend not to understand this?” (Deleuze & Guattari, 1972, p. 103)

#### Conclusions & Speculations
- Mostly makes the claim that the BwO is the thing that underlies these historical processes and the source from which these material flows emerge and circulate
    
- Also ends with a point that these processes can not be explained with a limited top-down conception of history (i.e. De Landa’s arguments against using -isms as a linear model for historical development) in favor of exploring the circulation of historical materials from the bottom-up

#### Introduction
De Landa's work opens with reflections on linear and nonlinear models and approaches to a variety of disciplines, whether in physics, mathematics, biology, chemistry, and makes the argument that the social sciences and humanities, and studies of history in particular, have too often focused on the development of linear theories to be applied from the top-down in explaining historical phenomena, such as the rise of cities and urban spaces. He argues that top-down models of history based around linear progress toward an optimal point depict the historical development of Western societies and their domination of oppressed societies in terms of a natural inclination toward progress and modernity, thus invisibilizing the more complex systems that give rise to European colonialism and imperialism. In other words, the primacy of linear causality in historical research erroneously frames such historical outcomes as natural.

Rather than presuming that historical outcomes are natural, then, De Landa argues in favor of a “bottom-up” approach to history, one that looks at historical development on various scales in such a way that is neither solely focused on individual subjects or their collective societies. To do so, he outlines the structure of his book as an experiment in this new materialist approach to history, looking at the emergence of historical outcomes as a process of accumulation of various kinds. Divided into three major parts, he looks at geological, biological, and linguistic accumulations throughout history. Notably, he explains that his interest in non-linearity does not mean that he will take a non-chronological approach to history; instead, he divides each section into three chapters, first tracing these accumulations from years 1000-1700 CE, then reflecting on the utility of this philosophy of history, and finally picking up his analysis again in years 1700 - 2000 CE.
