## Intervention, Argument, Evidence
### Intervention: What is the intervention that the scholar is making, and where is that intervention being made? In other words, how do you place that intervention relative to existing disciplinary conversations that are taking place?
Tamboukou Largely responding to the tendency to frame archives as the end of the historical life cycle for documents, often framed as the place where they can finally be put to a particular kind of research work , A framing of the archival research process that the author fears Risks and over adherence to _archival sensibility_ (Moore et al., 2016), which she states "encompasses a set of practices that highlight the need to study archival documents carefully, in the sense that they should not be simply treated as sources of nice quotations or as illustrations of an analysis that was not led by their study." To be clear, Tamboukou is not suggesting that the idea of archival sensibility is without its benefit. Rather, She's highlighting a concern with the risks of fully enforcing a mode of analysis that is bound to archival artifacts as texts: "Simply put, we cannot engage with documents of life while ignoring the life of documents within the archive and beyond."

### Argument: What are the core argument(s) being made in the text?
Overall, the author is arguing that archives should be seen as assemblages that are able to extend across or connect to multiple spacio temporal points, and given that the act of compiling or making sense of or interpreting the various items within a given archive or even across a multitude of archives is something that is embodied and interpretive on the part of the researcher, Tamboukou is arguing in a favor of a process approach to archive a research , especially as informed by the philosophy of Alfred Whitehead.

### Evidence: What evidence is used to support the argument(s)?
Tamboukou Discusses her own experiences as an archival researcher, especially working with feminist writings from the 19th century in Paris, France. In particular, she writes about her experience coming across a newspaper article written in 1832 in which Joséphine Félicité Milizet wrote "Women alone will say what freedom they want."

She explains how her affective connection to this particular line of writing is one that is shaped by her own experience as feminist scholar, one that almost bridges across time and space Create a joining between her as the researcher, the archival document, and the originating author of the text. She writes, " In thus seeking answers to my questions about the meaning of freedom amongst the editors of the first feminist newspaper, I've imagined their struggles, worries and agonies of publishing a newspaper written by women only, by remembering my own involvement in the feminist press, a century later. It is in this process that I have felt the author's desire 'to say what freedom they want'--the phrase that had 'accidentally captivated me in the archive."

In the final portion of the article, the author shifts to explain how these encounters with archival texts that lead to moments of entanglement between the researcher and their historical origin also entail a shift in orientation toward the œuvre à faire, or "work to be made" (Souriau 2009 -1956-). The notion of œuvre à faire, essentially, is that work is not made solely through preconceived notions or plans, but instead is something that Is made specifically through the process of working. In the context of Tamboukou's methodological argument, The idea here is that archival research does not rely on apriori standards for what The research entails , and instead archive a work should be understood in terms of its processual nature, Especially as it is often shaped by these affective moments of encounter with historical texts.
