# Reading Notes
## SQR3
### Survey: Skim the text and identify main ideas
- Todd explains her personal experience in contending with the uptake of the "ontological turn" in the humanities and social sciences, especially in STS and anthropology.

- Draws on a narrative experience of her attending a lecture from Bruno Latour, where she expected from his invoking of the concept of _Gaia_ that he might also speak to the Iñupiat notion of _Sila_, which is often understood as the lifeforce that animates. When he does not do so, however, she questions why some Indigenous perspectives are taken up in the academy while others are forgotten, and the tendency of non-Indigenous scholars to take up these concepts without a fully informed understanding of their situatedness in these ontoepistemologies.

- Much of the article is especially attentative to these issues in the context of Indigenous communities' stakes and expertise in response to anthropogenic climate change.

- She includes a second vignette with her response to an ASA event theme description that referenced the risks of ethnographic methodology "going native".

- She ultimately draws on these two vignettes and her analysis to argue for renewed attention to Indigenous scholarship in academia, beyond mere representation or the use of Indigenous terms nad concepts, in favor of confronting the very ideologies, dualisms, and systemic practices that enable the marginalization of Indigenous perspectives and thought to occur in the first place.

## Intervention, Argument, Evidence
### Intervention: What is the intervention that the scholar is making, and where is that intervention being made? In other words, how do you place that intervention relative to existing disciplinary conversations that are taking place?
Addressing epistemic injustic, especially when it contributes to ongoing colonial violence, in the academy, and in anthropology / social sciences in particular. This intervention is provoked by the author's encounter with scholarship that is, partially, reminiscent of Indigenous thought, practice, and scholarship, while not directly addressing institutional violence and the complicity of non-Indigenous scholars in continuously subjegating Indigenous onto-epistemologies.

### Argument: What are the core argument(s) being made in the text?
"So the argument I make here is that Indigenous peoples, throughout the world, are fighting for recognition - fighting to assert their laws, philosophies and stories on their own terms. When anthropologists and other assembled social scientists sashay in and start cherry-picking parts of Indigenous thought that appeal to them without engaging directly in (or unambiguously acknowledging) the political situation, agency, legal orders and relationality of both Indigenous people and scholars, we immediately become complicit in colonial violence. When we cite European thinkers who discuss the ‘more-than-human’ but do not discuss their Indigenous contemporaries who are writing on the exact same topics, we perpetuate the white supremacy of the academy. At the moment, it is by-and-large the academy that creates, legitimises and reproduces anthropology. However, for Indigenous academics like me, it is also the structures of academy that prevent the discipline from realising its loftiest, most transformative goals. The academy is anthropology’s ‘human error’: the white supremacist, Imperial human dimensions of the academy itself prevent the re-imagining of disciplines like anthropology" (p. 18).

### Evidence: What evidence is used to support the argument(s)?
- Use of two vignettes of the author's experiences in the academy to trace issues of epistemic injustice in the social sciences, especially in regards to a rejection of Indigenous world-building and ways of knowing

### How: What methods and theories are used to make the intervention or argument and support it?
