### Query: Write questions to consider as you read
- This reading is firmly rooted in decolonial scholarship, with Wynter notably being a protege of Carter G. Woodson and connecting her work with scholars such as Fanon and Quijano. Though this may seem as though it is a bit of a departure from "new materialist" perspectives, my primary concern here is to consider how Wynter confronts the subject/object and related dichotomies that have undergirded forms of white supremacy, and where does this intersect with the non-dualist ontology of new materialist scholarship?

- Where are there analogs or potential connections to the way that Puar puts legal, political, or other disciplinary interrogations of power (i.e. intersectionality theory) into conversation with new materialist theories (i.e. assemblages)? How can Wynter's work, and the work of other antiracist and decolonial scholars, be brought into the new materialist fold as well?

- Todd (2016) offers a critique of the uptake of the "ontological turn" in the humanities and social sciences due to a lack of attention to the nondualist ontologies that are not only fundamental to many Indigenous communities and their ontoepistemological perspectives, but that have been systematically and violently surpressed. Knowing this, could a similar critique of new materialist scholarship be made in relationship to the decolonial perspectives of scholars like Wynter?

Connection / synthesis reflection from CRD 701:

The Quijano article did a nice job of framing a major issue posed by the subject/object dichotomy that underpins so many European and Western ontological frameworks. He writes:

    And such inequalities are perceived as being of nature: only European culture is rational, it can contain ‘subjects’ – the rest are not rational, they cannot be or harbor ‘subjects’. As a consequence, the other cultures are different… they only can be ‘objects’ of knowledge or/and of domination practices (Quijano, 2007, p. 174).
    
This reminds me of Edward Said’s work on Orientalism and the issue of Othering cultures such that they are viewed as an object of study, rather than recognizing the capacity of all peoples to contribute to knowledge production and transmission. It seems that there are two mechanisms at play here: on the one hand, cultures outside of the West are viewed through the logic of biological racism, which is used to justify the idea that cultures that are Othered are inherently subordinate to Western European cultures. At the same time, in order to further colonial, imperial, or otherwise dominating sociopolitical and economic projects, acts of violence are used as a means to prevent the production and transmission of knowledge from people of the global majority. The issue of language and its relation to knowledge production comes to mind; for example, the forceful imposition of English in residential boarding schools, which led to the suppression of Indigenous languages and continues to have severe ramifications for maintaining these languages from generation to generation. In other words, it seems like there is a contradiction here, where these cultures are viewed as inherently inferior, and yet, severe action is taken to dominate and prevent the transmission of forms of knowledge production outside of the West.

Wynter’s article similarly critiques the ontological view that underpins this subordination: namely, the mapping of the subject/object dichotomy onto the man/human distinction. In other words, Western and European people are viewed as being capable of self-awareness, reflection, and thought, whereas people outside of these specific cultures are dehumanized, viewed as evolutionarily inferior and acting based on instinctual nature. As Wynter writes, “the West would therefore remain unable, from then on, to conceive of an Other to what it calls human—an Other, therefore, to its correlated postulates of power, truth, freedom” (2003, p. 280). Notably, however, Wynter traces a shift in this ontological view over time, describing the manner in which early Western thought relied on theology and views of inherent good/evil associated, in particular, with Christian thought, to later Western thought which weaponized Darwinian theory in order to argue that Westerners and Europeans were biologically superior as a process of evolutionary selection over time. In either case, these beliefs result in dehumanization that justifies the exploitation of, and theft from, Black and Indigenous people.

While Quijano and Wynter explore the ways in which the subject/object binary is a force underpinning the suppression of the epistemologies of people outside of the West, Cusicanqui offers a empowering look at autonomy and the capacity of those who experience marginalization to take hold of their contributions to history. She writes,

    “...we indigenous were and are, above all, contemporary beings and peers, and in this dimension [aka pacha], we perform and display our own commitment to modernity… The project of indigenous modernity can emerge from the present in a spiral whose movement is a continuous feedback from the past to the future—a ‘principle of hope’ or ‘anticipatory consciousness’–that both discerns and realizes decolonization at the same time” (2012, p. 96).

Rather than being relegated as non-contributors to history, or as existing in a linear past that fails to require ongoing action in decolonizing work, Cusicanqui argues that indigenous peoples are active modern figures who continue to shape the world through their ways of being, acting, and knowing. She argues that it is essential to move beyond linear evaluations of the causes and effects of colonization, but to recognize and participate in continuous resistance of its oppressive structures. This point, in particular, resonated for me: it seems that we can, as scholars, read, critique, respond to, and write about colonization endlessly, but ultimately, to have been given access to the resources and power that comes with being members of an academic institution requires that we utilize those tools to actively work toward decolonization in our own communities.

Because of the theme of “Othering” here and issues of epistemology, I contributed Orientalism, a 1977 article from Edward Said, to our alternative reading list, in which he argues:

	“...account of Orientalism would have to consider not only the professional
    Orientalist and his work, but also the very notion of a field of study based on a geographical, cultural, linguistic and ethnic unit called the Orient. Fields, of course, are made. They acquire coherence and integrity in time because scholars devote themselves in different ways to what seems to be a commonly agreed-upon subject matter” (p. 162)

Here, Said suggests that we can consider peoples who are, based on Western and European definitions, located within the “Orient,” as not just objects of study, but as pieces that make up an entire field or discipline tasked with viewing, labeling, and describing the cultures and people that it encompasses. In this article, he explains the development of Orientalism as a field, and he explains the manner in which its scholars use their institutional power and status to make claims about these cultures, regardless of their validity. By relegating those located within the “Orient” to places that are ideologically framed as distant or rooted in the past relative to their Western and European counterparts, scholars are able to suppress their ability to represent themselves as they have been positioned as epistemologically inferior. Overall, this reading offers a commentary that is in some ways similar to that of Wynters and Quijan from the lens of Said’s larger body of work on Orientalism.

One thing that I found myself curious about was the use of the term “Judeo-Christian” in Wynter’s article. I was not sure if that was a term more common in the early-2000s, or if it has always been controversial in its use, but I was curious to know if there was context I was missing there as to why Wynter uses that term as opposed to others? Is she trying to mark theological beliefs that are common to Judaism and Christianity, or does she use that term in the more recent political sense, particularly as it is understood in the context of the United States? Most recently, the term seems to retain usage in far-right spaces as a way to denote populist and evangelical beliefs, Othering anyone who is viewed outside of these political-religious goals. Is Wynter mobilizing this term to refer to that populist ideology, or something else?
	
Furthermore, I was curious about the meaning of the “arboreal structure of internal colonialism” (Cusicanqui, 2012, p. 101). She mentions that universities, organizations, etc. are institutions that operate within, and as part of, larger colonial structures, but is the “arboreal” metaphor meant to signal a hierarchical structure or division? Is it organized from the top-down or from the bottom-up? I appreciated the idea that there’s multiple branches and layers, a notable aspect of bureaucratic institutions, such that when you have some kind of issue within an institution, it seems like you get tossed from one “branch” to the next, until you ultimately give up, as the power appears to have no center and therefore be difficult to organize against. Or, maybe she is referring more closely to the establishment of academic fields and disciplines which, in establishing a focus on (post/de-)colonial studies, remove access to the theory and practice of decolonization that would support the very people they focus on as objects of study. Ultimately, I’m curious about the analogy and how we can use it to understand the ways in which power is organized in these institutions.
