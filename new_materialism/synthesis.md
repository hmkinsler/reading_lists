# Introducing New Materialist Thought
There are a variety of scholarly turns that are identified as the source of necessary response that is characteristic of new materialist scholarship, several of which are cited by the authors in this reading list, including Kuhl and Frost's claim that new materialism aids in responding to the linguistic focus or representationalist tendencies of the cultural turn or the limitations of the linguistic turn, as Haraway describes it. But ultimately, new materialism is characterized by an interest in taking up analysis of the active manner in which matter plays a role in shaping the world and the many dimensions of human and social life located within it. In doing so, new materialist scholarship seeks to respond to and break down a number of dualisms, including that of Cartesian dualism, the mind-body divide, the nature-culture divide, the social and science divide, the subject-object divide, the human and non-human divide. And Haraway (1981) offers her Cyborg figure as a kind of ironic representation of the messiness that comes from crossing these boundaries or sitting from within the borderlands and the messiness of bringing all of these otherwise thought of disparate dimensions of the world together. Coole and Frost (2010) outline a variety of focuses and commitments that are specific to new materialist scholarship, including its attention to the vitalist or active and agentic or energetic capacities of the material world and matter in ways that help to break down the divide between humans and non-human entities, often considering the latter of which from the lens of being more than human as opposed to lacking in human-ness. They further note that this work is interested in returning to problems of biopower as it is understood and picked up from the work of scholars like Michel Foucault to consider how a new materialist perspective invites us to reconsider the role of humans in a world where human actions have ethical consequences that extend beyond impacts on non-living entities, which thus shapes and shifts our roles and responsibilities in interacting with the ecological and relational milieus in which we as humans are situated. Finally, Coole and Frost (2010) explain that new materialist scholarship maintains the political economical focus that is expressed in reference to the materialist scholarship on which it draws. Here they explained that an interest in the vitality of matter or a shift toward an ontological understanding of the material world as being active and resistive to human control and domain is not such that humans are fully relinquishing their responsibility, but instead calls our attention to the more complex variety of entities or aggregate of materials and things that come together in a variety of ways to produce different kinds of social and political arrangements.

Some of the core characteristics or tenets that define new materialist scholarship, then, include:

1. An affirmation of matter as lively, vital, dynamic, and active, rather than inert or passive.

2. A rejection of a Cartesian dualist ontology that places humans as active subjects as though they are the sole force that enacts agency over the natural materal world as the passive recipient of their action.

3. Renewed attention to issues of human ethics and responsibility in the midst of a greater ecology in which we are but one active participant.

As Mitchell (2002) explains, in social theory especially, there has been a longstanding tension to view historical change through singular lenses or frames. In his analysis of the ecological networks involved in the spread of malaria in Egypt and beyond via mosquitos in the late 19th and early 20th centuries, he explains that historical accounts of this time period have produced many isolated histories, agricultural histories of sugar cane production in the region, histories of the electrical infrastructure development via dams and hydroelectric power, or economic histories tracing the accumulation of power and capital to prominent landowners and political figures at that time. As he notes, however, none of these narratives manage to bring all of these interrelated entities together to offer a more complex role of the many forces that shaped Egypt's history at this time. This, for him, is the problem that comes with the "age of experts," or human actors who view themselves as the central determining force for controlling these aspects of the so-called natural world, despite the vitalist nature of these materials being enacted through their resistance, their unexpected behavior, and the myriad ways in which humans' ability to exercise power over matter was exceeded by its own agency. Ultimately, new materialism is located in understanding these limitations in scholars' attempts to account for the events and unfoldings of the material world and our relationship to it by giving materiality its due.
