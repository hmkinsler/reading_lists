# Patterns from previous CRDM exam questions
- Instructions to synthesize and put multiple readings into conversation with one another, considering where they may converge or diverge, how a disciplinary conversation changes over time, or provide models for mapping out a disciplinary conversation that is taking place.
- Draw on readings to apply to a given case study.
- Explain how the readings are informing your proposed research for the dissertation, especially to defend the feasability, theoretical framework, and methodology you intend to engage in your work, as well as what you see as the intervention or contribution your research will make to the field.
- Compare and contrast between theoretical perspectives from multiple scholars, offering synthesis or your own argumentative perspective in response.
- Define major theories, clarify conceptual definitions, and identify key themes covered in the readings.
- Identify limitations and gaps or areas for future research in a given disciplinary area based on your readings.
- Extrapolate and explain some of the choices made in writing your exam rationales.
- Describe several key studies from the reading lists that you believe are exemplars of this scholarship and clearly explain what they do well, methodologically and theoretically.

# Considerations for exam process
- Ensure that there is access to voice-to-text technologies to assist with writing
- Identify which writing software(s) or any other writing materials will be useful to include for the written exams
- Consider whether extra time is needed to make use of accessibility tools
- Multimodal writing? Writing by hand? Access to drafting paper and / or materials like whiteboards?

# Approaches for note taking
- Develop thematic maps to help synthesize the research you are reading-- you might develop groups by topic, theoretical / methodological approach, or historical debates unfolding in the field
- Continuously tie back the readings to your research, asking how they inform or intersect with your proposed topic
- Create your own questions based on the lists and practice answering them without notes
- Draw on note-taking models that aid in tracking the major arguments of each source (i.e. SQR3, intervention / evidence / argument)
- Even when focusing on a specific chapter(s) from a book, you should briefly read through and skim through the rest of the text, especially the introduction and conclusion sections, to understand where the argument that the author(s) hone in on within that specific section are situated in relation to the broader context of the text

# Strategies for writing exam responses
- Split up your time across the four hours (240 minutes) into manageable chunks to keep things on track:
    - **Review the Question** (15 minutes) | Read question carefully and make note of what you are being asked to respond to. Note the verbs directing what you need to do (i.e. synthesize, compare, apply) and the constraints you are being given. Make note of which scholars or readings on your list imediately come to mind as useful in responding to the question.
    - **Brainstorm** (30 minutes) | Write out all of the key concepts, citations, major arguments and interventions, anything that comes to mind from your list. This will help you sort through the possible lines of response up front, and it can be helpful to organize things around the major sections of the list.
    - **Outline and Thesis** (30 minutes) | Draft up thesis response to the question and structure the outline for your paper based on your argument. You are using all of the information from your brainstorm to develop this outline, considering how many body sections you want to cover in the paper (3-5), and consider what points will anchor each section. Which readings will you cover in each section? Where will you tie in different elements of the question's directions (i.e. application to your own dissertation research, analysis of a case study)?
   - **Draft** (145 minutes) | You want to divide your time between each section equally. Write the sections you feel the most comfortable with first-- this need not be a linear process! Importantly, you want to focus on maintaining forward momentum as you write, rather than getting everything perfect.
    - **Conclusion** (20 minutes) | Ensure that you everything back to the central thesis or argument of the paper. This is your area to answer the "so what?" question for your readers, which allows you to consider gaps in your response, answers for further exploration, etc. As a PhD student who is staking out your role in your field, this is your opportunity to really cement the intervention that you are interested in making with your work.
    - **Proofread** (10 minutes) | Read things over, check that you cited what you intended to, check for alignment between your opening thesis and what ultimately is discussed in the body sections and conclusion of your paper.

# Sample practice question drills

## Information Retrieval and Argument Building | 30 minutes
- Review question and brainstorm (15 minutes)
- Outline and thesis (15 minutes)

## Single Section Approach | 60 - 90 minutes
- Review question and brainstorm (10-15 minutes)
- Outline and thesis (10-15 minutes)
- Draft a single section (i.e. 2-5 pages) (40-60 minutes)

## Scaled Mock Exam | 120 minutes
- Review the question (5 minutes)
- Brainstorm (10 minutes)
- Outline and thesis (15 minutes)
- Draft around 8-10 pages (80 minutes)
- Conclusion (10 minutes)
