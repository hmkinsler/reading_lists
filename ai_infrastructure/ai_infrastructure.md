# AI Infrastructure

## Rationale
This list takes up scholarship in critical infrastructure studies, considering how the very nature of infrastructure is such that, as Star notes, “is by definition invisible, part of the background for other kinds of work” (1999, p. 38). In response to her call to understand the underlying systems that shape the norms and practices of technological users, I draw here on scholarship centered around critical interrogation of the underlying data structures, information systems, and materials that serve as the infrastructures for AI technologies, thus taking AI as an object for critical study. In doing so, I seek to understand what kinds of infrastructural literacy are required for humanities researchers to bring these otherwise invisible systems back to the forefront as a necessary act to be able to repair, rewire, and resist AI technologies. The first section of the list draws on this core scholarship in critical infrastructure studies for this reason, notably including the work of Star, Bratton, and Bowker, to ground this framework for theorizing about AI technologies and systems. 

The second section looks to the infrastructural components that comprise AI technologies, considering the role of power and bias in their development. At a time when concerns about the ethical implications of AI technologies and systems abound, including their impact on the environment and local water supplies, the inequitable extraction of minerals and resources from communities in the global South, and the privacy concerns that arise from the massive collection of data that is used to train many of these AI models, this section explores the infrastructures that underlie and render AI technology possible. In other words, this sublist seeks to interrogate the means by which the infrastructure underlying AI has become naturalized or normalized despite the power dynamics that frequently shape its development, maintenance, and operation.

The third section then considers the effects of AI’s integration into public infrastructure, with careful consideration of the implications for surveillance, data extraction, or the enforcement of power on people who encounter these technologies. Readings in this sublist include analyses of several infrastructural technologies that rely on or otherwise integrate AI into their systems. For example, several articles explore the development and emergence of Flock Safety cameras, or license plate recognition cameras that have spread to municipalities across the United States. These cameras are used to log the movement of not only motor vehicles that traverse public roadways, but they also have been found to include motion tracking algorithms and facial recognition technologies. Consequently, they serve as a massive surveillance network, raising the question of what risks and implications for privacy, autonomy, and freedom of thought, speech, and movement are posed by the deployment of AI technologies as infrastructure.

Finally, the last section explores the emergence of agentic AI and its potential consequences for critical infrastructure studies in the future. As agentic AI technologies are enabled with the capacity to autonomously make decisions, in many cases with humans “out of the loop,” this sublist seeks to understand what the future of AI technologies’ integration into infrastructure might look like when AI agents are given the capacity to act on behalf of people.
