### Recite: Consider what you want to remember, and put that into your own words
The author here seems interested in defining the aspects that make agentic AI technologies distinct from their earlier autonomous AI counterparts, the former of which is most characteristically defined bye the ability to independently set and manage its own goals. Tiwari acknowledges that the deployment of Agentic AI technologies in urban spaces is emergent, but he sees some of the risks associated with these technologies as severe enough to warrant anticipatory regulatory action and ethical analysis before the shift from existing "smart city" models to post-smart urbanism fully takes hold.

### Relate: Identify connections or links to other readings and scholarship
There are a lot of clear connections between issues of governance that are raised in this article to those that are so predominant in Bratton's (2016) analysis. In particular, Bratton's discussion of the city layer of the stack has the clearest alignment here-- interest in how agentic AI as a form of computation leads to new forms of urban spatiality, urban governance, and sovereignty.

This link between governance, geopolitics, and spatiality seems to be a really common theme for critical urban studies , especially in the context of analyzing recent or emergent technologies. It is notable to me that this article and its close counterpart in Shariatpour & Behzadfar (2026) Have both been published so recently, and the concerns that are outlined in both of their articles align with my own observations pertaining to the deployment of surveillance infrastructure or surveillance technologies in my own city .

## Qualifying Exam Relevance
The idea of "co-governance" expressed in this paper is really important to interrogate further as we seek to develop regulation models, forms of oversight, and safety and ethical guidelines and frameworks that help us mitigate harm in the development and use of agentic AI models, tools, and systems, especially when integrated as part of urban infrastructure. As Tiwari puts it, "Agentic Urban AI presents a significant disruption in conventional models of governance by introducing autonomous systems capable of independently prioritizing and adapting urban outcomes, frequently operating beyond the scope of direct human oversight. Scholars have identified a range of associated challenges, including issues of attribution, accountability, system interoperability, and algorithmic bias. Effectively addressing these complexities necessitates a comprehensive re-evaluation of existing governance structures, regulatory mechanisms, and ethical frameworks to ensure the responsible and equitable integration of AI into urban systems" (p. 11).

There are some ways in which the manner that agency is discussed in this article produces some new problems for the way that we think about governance of technological systems, because the idea of agency here is specifically mentioned as distributive, but this seems to be a description of the activity of these systems as opposed to something about their ontological status. In other words, distributed agency, as Tiwari is describing it, seems to refer more so to the idea that the ability to independently set goals and act on those goals is a capability that is now shared across agentic AI systems and humans alike, and thus becomes more complex as urban activities are networked between human and more than human actors or actants. I do, however, think that this is a framing of AI that requires a bit more hedging in terms of its ontological status, because, as Bennett argues in her 2010 book, it is precisely because agency is distributed that we have to think critically about ethics and the placement of responsibility in moments where technologies and infrastructure push back or act or fail in unanticipated and sometimes harmful ways, as was the case with the North American blackout of 2003. In other words, I think that an important way to contextualize some of the argument that Tawari is making is perhaps to look at the idea of reconfiguration that Suchman raises in terms of the relationship between humans and machines. Understanding that their differential boundaries are asymmetrical and that we need to understand the idea of ethical responsibility according to the power asymmetries that exist between AI systems or agentic AI systems and tools and their human counterparts. I think that this is a really important theoretical underpinning or assumption that we have to carry with us if we're going to think about co-governance because it could be too readily treated as though agentic AI is acting largely or fully of its own accord in precisely the manner of technological determinism that Winner warns us against. Now at the same time, Winner has his reasons for understanding that some technologies are inherently political in their design and function and operation and use. But ultimately, a social determinist model of technology is additionally not fully sufficient for capturing the relationship that humans have to these technologies and systems in their design, operation, maintenance, installation, and so on and so forth. Ultimately, I think a co-governance model could work, and I don't inherently disagree with Tiwari's point here, but I would likely press for a deeper connection between co-governance as an ethical framework and the kinds of ontological or relational assumptions that underlie that framework in terms of understanding the relationship between human societies, social values, and the design and consequences of these technological systems.

At one point, Tiwari makes the statement that agency should be understood as not just an extension of the capabilities of automated systems. In other words, agency is not the linear progressive result of automation in terms of the progression of computational technologies and their development. Instead, he makes the argument that agentic AI systems and agentic models and their architecture and their development and their functioning are constitutive of a paradigmatic shift in the way that we think about machine learning and how these models are designed and meant to operate and be integrated as part of the infrastructure of other information and communication technologies and systems or networks. And I think that this is a really true statement that speaks to just how pressing of an issue this is for researchers in science and technology studies and in the humanities and social sciences as a whole. Because if we're thinking about what our role is as people who are studying dimensions of human and social experience and attempting to account for the role that we have in designing these technologies and the way that these technologies design us in our worlds back, then we need to think about this paradigmatic shift in computation overall and the way that that's going to impact us in terms of our scholarship, our methodologies, our pedagogy. It is hard to overstate just how drastic of a change this kind of a model for developing computational systems and tools is. And they're becoming increasingly modular too. I recently went to the American Historical Association's conference on AI and history and I learned about harnesses for AI systems and the way that open weight models can be plugged into these harnesses and the way that agents are being deployed locally or via model context protocols. And I began to experiment with some of these tools and systems on my own for the first time and I was really quite astounded by how rapidly these tools are being developed, specifically in their ability to interface with each other. The world of interoperability is changing and there are increasingly instances of various software packages and products and human video games being reverse engineered in ways that allow for new kinds of remixing to occur. And so it is really quite remarkable how dramatic the shift has been in the way that we interface with computation in the last five or so years.
