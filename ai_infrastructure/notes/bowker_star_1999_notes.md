### Survey: Skim the text and identify main ideas
- Intro: The introduction for Bowker and Starr's book really helps to outline the stakes of their interrogation into systems of classification, considering how defining a category and placing any kind of person or object or animal under the label of that category is an ontological action.

- Ch. 1: The first chapter outlines a framework for understanding and identifying and analyzing systems of classification and standardization. They explain the role of classification systems in infrastructure, making the argument that classification is an aspect of our methodological considerations for critical infrastructure studies. 

- Ch. 2: They explore the rising use of classification and standardization systems in the late 19th century , considering the close relationship between systemic standardization and the Industrial Revolution. They look at how this interest in classification found its way into scientific research practice, the design of public spaces, and was influential in shaping human social behavior .

- Ch. 3: They then take up the International Classification of Diseases (ICD) As a case study for classification systems that emerged in the 19th century. they make use of archival resources to understand how classification strategies have shifted every decade since the ICD was first established up to the present day.

- Ch. 4: They then consider how the use of classification systems and the way they are materialized in medical record documents has been responded to by medical insurance companies , especially in the case of life expectancy measures, pre existing conditions, and the inclusion or exclusion of contextual factors that could lead to illness, such as exposure to toxic materials and chemicals or unsafe working conditions.

- Ch. 5: The second part of the book is largely interested in individual experiences of the consequences of classification systems, especially as these experiences are described through biography. Chapter five thus is focused on the experience of tuberculosis patients in response to the manner in which their disease has been classified. 

- Ch. 6: In maintaining this focus on lived experiences of the consequences of classification, Bowker and Starr consider how race classification and reclassification in South Africa during and after the apartheid regime shaped their experiences of segregation in multiple ways, whether in terms of how they navigate public space, access to education and employment, Or their experience of various societal institutions .

- Ch. 7: This part of the book then shifts into understanding the relationship between work practices and classification, with the 7th chapter looking at how nursing scientists at the University of Iowa sought to develop a classification system for nursing intervetions, or the practices that nurses carry out On a daily basis as part of their work.

- Ch. 8: Here, the authors consider the relationship between classification in memory, especially in instances where institutions are prone to organizational forgetting. they consider what can be historically revealed in looking at the historicity of classification systems, As well as the various factors that lead to The maintenance or loss of various forms of knowledge.

- Ch. 9: Considers the relationship between language and classification systems , especially to consider how individual language use may or may not be in tension with the categories that are outlined in such systems as people seek to describe "the complexities of people, things, and their relationships" (pp. 283-284).

- Ch. 10: This chapter focuses on " a discussion of the ways in which research and classification and standards can inform a larger program of research into the building and maintaining of infrastructure---and its simultaneous material-cultural nature" (p. 284).

##### Introduction

Notes from encountering reading in my Intro to Digital Humanities course:

    **Discussion Prompt:** What do you take away from this piece as important when thinking about classification?  
    
    **Response:** I think this example aptly highlights how there are often not easy solutions when it comes to classification. In this instance, with either outcome, there is a concern that a group experiencing marginalization is rendered invisible in some form or another. When I think about data and classification, especially when it comes to research in the humanities, seeking out all of the possible outcomes is really key particularly because researchers do not want to mistakenly find themselves too far from the material circumstances in which people live. A similar discussion comes up with the issue of pathologizing transness, for example, and the inclusion of gender dysphoria as a clinical diagnosis in the DSM. On the one hand, like many other trans and nonbinary people, I firmly believe that there is a long history of anti-trans sentiment embedded in its medicalization and the resulting classifying of trans people as having a psychological disorder. At the same time, that diagnosis is a necessary step for accessing insurance benefits, being able to get appropriate medical care, and so on, so I could never imagine myself advocating for the removal of that classification until we first see a dramatic shift in our medical institutions’ handling of care for trans people. Ultimately, I think what is important, then, is that we have to remember that there are these systemic issues that underlie and shape social life, and that can often mean that there are no simple or easy answers when it comes to classification.
