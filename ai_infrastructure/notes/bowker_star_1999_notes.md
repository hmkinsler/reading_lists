### Survey: Skim the text and identify main ideas
- Intro: The introduction for Bowker and Starr's book really helps to outline the stakes of their interrogation into systems of classification, considering how defining a category and placing any kind of person or object or animal under the label of that category is an ontological action.

- Ch. 1: The first chapter outlines a framework for understanding and identifying and analyzing systems of classification and standardization. They explain the role of classification systems in infrastructure, making the argument that classification is an aspect of our methodological considerations for critical infrastructure studies. 

- Ch. 2: They explore the rising use of classification and standardization systems in the late 19th century , considering the close relationship between systemic standardization and the Industrial Revolution. They look at how this interest in classification found its way into scientific research practice, the design of public spaces, and was influential in shaping human social behavior .

- Ch. 3: They then take up the International Classification of Diseases (ICD) As a case study for classification systems that emerged in the 19th century. they make use of archival resources to understand how classification strategies have shifted every decade since the ICD was first established up to the present day.

- Ch. 4: They then consider how the use of classification systems and the way they are materialized in medical record documents has been responded to by medical insurance companies , especially in the case of life expectancy measures, pre existing conditions, and the inclusion or exclusion of contextual factors that could lead to illness, such as exposure to toxic materials and chemicals or unsafe working conditions.

- Ch. 5: The second part of the book is largely interested in individual experiences of the consequences of classification systems, especially as these experiences are described through biography. Chapter five thus is focused on the experience of tuberculosis patients in response to the manner in which their disease has been classified. 

- Ch. 6: In maintaining this focus on lived experiences of the consequences of classification, Bowker and Starr consider how race classification and reclassification in South Africa during and after the apartheid regime shaped their experiences of segregation in multiple ways, whether in terms of how they navigate public space, access to education and employment, Or their experience of various societal institutions .

- Ch. 7: This part of the book then shifts into understanding the relationship between work practices and classification, with the 7th chapter looking at how nursing scientists at the University of Iowa sought to develop a classification system for nursing intervetions, or the practices that nurses carry out On a daily basis as part of their work.

- Ch. 8: Here, the authors consider the relationship between classification in memory, especially in instances where institutions are prone to organizational forgetting. they consider what can be historically revealed in looking at the historicity of classification systems, As well as the various factors that lead to The maintenance or loss of various forms of knowledge.

- Ch. 9: Considers the relationship between language and classification systems , especially to consider how individual language use may or may not be in tension with the categories that are outlined in such systems as people seek to describe "the complexities of people, things, and their relationships" (pp. 283-284).

- Ch. 10: This chapter focuses on " a discussion of the ways in which research and classification and standards can inform a larger program of research into the building and maintaining of infrastructure---and its simultaneous material-cultural nature" (p. 284).

In this instance, with either outcome, there is a concern that a group experiencing marginalization is rendered invisible in some form or another. When I think about data and classification, especially when it comes to research in the humanities, seeking out all of the possible outcomes is really key particularly because researchers do not want to mistakenly find themselves too far from the material circumstances in which people live. A similar discussion comes up with the issue of pathologizing transness, for example, and the inclusion of gender dysphoria as a clinical diagnosis in the DSM. On the one hand, like many other trans and nonbinary people, I firmly believe that there is a long history of anti-trans sentiment embedded in its medicalization and the resulting classifying of trans people as having a psychological disorder. At the same time, that diagnosis is a necessary step for accessing insurance benefits, being able to get appropriate medical care, and so on, so I could never imagine myself advocating for the removal of that classification until we first see a dramatic shift in our medical institutions’ handling of care for trans people. Ultimately, I think what is important, then, is that we have to remember that there are these systemic issues that underlie and shape social life, and that can often mean that there are no simple or easy answers when it comes to classification.

##### Chapter One | Some Tricks of the Trade in Analyzing Classification
This chapter helps to more explicitly elucidate some of the differences between classification, infrastructure, and standardization, or the relationship between all three of these things to one another. They provide a definition of infrastructure (p. 35), which can be explored in greater detail in Star (1999), characterized by:
    - Embeddedness
    - Transparency
    - Reach or scope
    - Learned as part of membership
    - Links with conventions of practice
    - Embodiment of standards
    - Built on an installed base
    - Becomes visible upon breakdown
    - Is fixed in modular increments, not all at once or globally

they explained that the invisible and embedded or transparent nature of infrastructure must necessarily be combated by infrastructural inversion, or a conscious investment in understanding how infrastructures are developed and maintained. They explore four methodological themes of infrastructural inversion, including:
    - Ubiquity of classifying and standardization
    - The materiality and texture of classifications and standards-- they are "_material_, as well as symbolic"
    - The indeterminancy of the past, or a multivocal perspective on history
    - The practical politics of classifying an dstandardizing

#### Part I: Classification and Large-Scale Infrastructures

##### Chapter Two | The Kindness of Strangers: Kinds and Politics in Classification Systems
They begin reading of the politics that are encoded in the ICD, exploring how the use of this medical classification system has changed over time, particularly as a result of the negotiation of the prototypical standards that the classification scheme Seeks to impose on its users in relationship to the Aristotelian or local contextual use of the system in practice. they look at the contextual use of the ICD in classifying causes of death so that mortality statistics could be measured both nationally and internationally, Considering how these local practices at times were in competition with one another, and the attempts of the designers of the ICD to account for these divergent practices as much as possible in future iterations of the ICD.

##### Chapter Three | The ICD as Information Infrastructure
They continue their discussion of the ICD as an information infrastructure That relies on the collection of data. In other words, the ICD is not only a classification system, But it also relies on practices of standardization and measurement in order to count number and types of classifications that are applied from the scheme in any given circumstance, whether that has to do with collecting data about the number of deaths associated with a given illness across time or across space, or with the manner in which that data is utilize by medical insurance companies, and so on. They highlight some of the power dynamics that are at play in this attempt to impose standardization, classification, and data collection practices on a global scale, Such as the concerns shared by medical practitioners in the global S who feel that the ICD is not focused on their immediate local, or material, concerns, leading them to question why their efforts are being directed toward data collection that will most likely disproportionately benefit countries in the global North or in the West that are designing and imposing these classification systems in the first place. The link between classification, standardization, enforcement, power, and surveillance are described as follows:

    The expanding wave of information gathering practices is a defining characteristic of the modern state, as Foucault (1991) observes. To produce and maintain standardized medical records, state bureaucrats needed to create a uniform set of data-gathering and encoding practices. Without these practices, standardization could not be achieved. These standards entailed a range of governmental activities, including accustoming citizens to the regular collection of information about ever more detailed aspects of their personal lives. Standards also meant enforcing a standardized set of procedures (p. 117).

##### Chapter Four | Classification, Coding, and Coordination
They extend their concern on the local utilization of the ICD by considering how it has been employed by several different groups, Including international public health agencies, national and local governments, doctors, epidemiologists, statisticians, and various industry actors. They explain how this singular classification system both seeks to centralized the efficacy of its use across these heterogeneous interestS At the same time that these special interest groups likewise seek to employ or shape the design of the ICD in ways that meet their specific needs. Once again, they look at the use of the ICD in the practice of death certification for the purpose of collecting mortality data , the need for which is stressed by international public health agencies and statisticians or national leaders, but which is viewed by physicians as a an unnecessary Impediment to their time caring for and treating living patients. As globalization leads to a greater scale of use for these classification systems and, correspondingly, greater instances of conflict or tension between the various groups that are using any given classification system or standard, the authors provide two primary lessons gleaned from their consideration of the ICD as a central coordinating information system across these groups:

    1. It is unrealistic and counterproductive to try to destroy all uncertainty and ambiguity in these sorts of infrastructural tools. By their very nature, classification systems need appropriate degrees of both to work--only within a totally uniform world (within a given speciality) would it even be conceivable to try to impose total precision... Rather than root out all instances of ambiguity, Analysts of standardized lists should instead seek clearly and consistently to define the degree of ambiguity that is appropriate to the object in question.

    2. No such tool can be defined once and for all. They are always the products of continuing negotiation and change... (pp. 157-158).

#### Part II: Classification and Biography, or System and Suffering

##### Chapter Five | Of Tuberculosis and Trajectories
Their next two chapters shift to consider how the impacts of classification systems are experienced, looking at two biographical case studies. In their first case study, which is the focus of this chapter, they consider how patients with tuberculosis respond to classification systems or measurement practices, especially in the context of Institutions in the 19th century that were used to isolate and surveil patients during the course of their treatment. For example, they discuss a character from Mann's _The Magic Mountain_ (1929) Who becomes acclimatized to the institution where she is being held and attempts to extend her treatment by manipulating a thermometer that she is being monitored with to falsely give the appearance of her having a fever, As well as another character who attempts to refuse the authority of the medical measurement devices he is being tracked with.

##### Chapter Six | The Case of Race Classification and Reclassification under Apartheid
They continue on this biographical exploration, shifting to a discussion of the use of classification and reclassification in the context of South Africa's apartheid . They demonstrate how these classifications change over time, often in contradictory ways. In one instance, they consider how a young woman who was born with a medical condition that impacted the texture of her hair and her skin tone in ways that we're visibly racialize differently from both of her white parents L to her classification in a different racial category than her family members, impacting her ability to continue attending her schools or exist in the segregated spaces that she had occupied prior to her reclassification.

#### Part III: Classification and Work Practice

##### Chapter Seven | What a Difference a Name Makes---the Classification of Nursing Work
They then consider how classification relates to work practice, Looking at the design of the NIC system, which sought to classify and make trackable the various practices that are specific to nursing. They explain how the NIC group that sought to develop this system was tasked with navigating the potential benefits and risks That's such a classification system would pose, Such as the benefits of making NIC practices comparable on a larger scale across all contacts where nursing work is taking place, or the benefits of rendering nursing practices as visible and therefore distinct and legitimate, The latter of which also runs the risk of enabling datafication and surveillance or tracking of nursing practices in ways that could worsen their labor conditions.

##### Chapter Eight | Organizational Forgetting, Nursing Knowledge, and Classification
The problem of the tension between perceived benefits and risks for the NIC Group continue on in terms of how nursing knowledge is remembered, or intentionally forgotten. 

#### Part IV: The Theory and Practice of Classifications

##### Chapter Nine | Categorical Work and Boundary Infrastructures: Enriching Theories of Classification
The authors seek to further explicate some of the relational concerns that they have identified in earlier portions of the book to help formulate a more complex and contextually tuned theory for classification and infrastructure, especially by building on the concept of _boundary objects_ by considering how they become scaled up as _boundary infrastructures_:

    These relations define a space against which and into information technologies of all sorts enter. These technologies of representation are entering into all sorts of communities of practice on a global scale, in design and in use. They are medium of communication and broadcast as well as of standardization. The toughest problems in information systems design are increasingly those concerned with modeling cooperation across heterogeneous worlds, of modeling articulation work and multiplicity (p. 308)

### Argument: What are the core argument(s) being made in the text?
Classification systems...
    - are infrastructural (Star, 1999)
    - require negotiation of the prototypical meanings that they seek to impose with their Aristotelian use in practice
    - are both shaped by social systems and shape those social systems in turn
    - require careful coordination across multiple heterogenous communities of practice, especially when deployed on larger scales
    - can render particular kinds of things visible or invisible, with both acts carrying their own unique kinds of benefits and risks
