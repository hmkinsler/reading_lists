### Survey: Skim the text and identify main ideas
- Intro: Eubanks starts off By describing her own experience grappling with an insurance company that likely had flagged her as a potential risk for fraud despite her immense need for financial support through submitting her claims, and she connects this personal lived experience to the experiences of many low income or working people across the United States who find themselves implicated in material consequences of algorithmic decision making, whether through EBT data collection and tracking, Risk assessment models employed by Child Welfare programs , and electronic registries documenting members of the unhoused community. She highlights the immense violence perpetuated by such surveillance systems, arguing that "across the country, poor and working-class people are targeted by new tools of digital poverty management and face life threatening consequences as a result" (p. 15).

- CH 1: She opens her first chapter by exploring the history of poor houses, which were used in cities across the United States throughout the 19th and early 20th centuries. These were institutions that Use to isolate low income people, especially using them as a source for manual labor. She considers how the poorhouse model has since been Transformed into modern public service institutions that make use of data collection and surveillance practices to maintain control over the populations that they serve, helping to maintain there low income status and perpetuate cycle of poverty, all the while using them as a site of extraction in the process.

- CH 2: This chapter focuses on a welfare programme in Indiana that was attended to aid streamlining welfare eligibility cheques, which ultimately led to many at risk residents being wrongfully kicked off of the Medicaid programme. She looks at a case study for a family with a daughter with numerous chronic health conditions, and she traces difficulties that they faced in attempting to reinstate their membership the Medicaid programme. Through no fault of their own, due to the manner and timeline in which they were kicked out of the programme due to this automated algorithm and the numerous bureaucratic barriers that they had to overcome in order to even be able to identify and begin to address the issue, they ultimately became ineligible to reinstate their membership Due to legislative membership restrictions. as she looks at several other examples of individuals who who shared in this experience.

- CH 3: This chapter focuses specifically on the conditions of homelessness in Los Angeles, California. Here, she looks at the use of a coordinated entry system that is advertised as helping match unhoused people with potential resources in the area . She looks at the historical development Of Skid Row following the early 20th century, particularly through the intentions segregation of low income people and planning development practices that restricted residents access to necessary services, accommodations, and amenities. She explains that ultimately results in the contemporary conditions of Skid Row, of which the coordinated entry system is intended to help remedy, But instead of requires participants to sign over personal data such as their Private health information to more than 150 agencies, including the Los Angeles Police Department . She considers how the coordinated entry system thus not only fails to address issues of homelessness or poverty in Los Angeles, but it also enables the perpetuation of systemic oppression and violence against the city's unhoused population.

- CH 4: This chapter takes as a case study the Allegheny Family Screening Tool (AFST) Use for intake screening by the county's child neglect and abuse hotline. She considers how some of the classifications for distinguishing abuse versus neglect are not fully or readily captured by the screening tool , especially as many case workers are tasked with distinguishing between instances where a family may not have as ready access to everyday needs for reasons that are out of their control, such as poverty, in instances where children are willfully being deprived of the necessities of everyday life. How caseworkers navigate their use of the screening tool, as well as its development in relationship to numerous local agencies focused on child welfare. 

- CH 5: She describes the use of these algorithmic surveillance systems as a kind of digital poor house that intentionally directs low income people away from the public services and resources that would facilitate their upward mobility. She provides several key characteristics of these digital poor houses , including their scalability, their complexity, their pervasiveness and persistence, They're intentionally developed longevity, and the membership of all citizens within this digital extractive system, regardless of whether or not someone is actively being subject to the violent decision making of those algorithms. 

- Conclusion: Her conclusion is forward looking, Considering how these algorithmic and surveillance tools that make up the digital poor house might be dismantled. She looks to civil rights movements in the 1960s, Citing the efforts of Martin Luther King Junior and the Poor People's campaign, especially for their focus on Developing class consciousness and understanding of the material circumstances that low income Americans face. She ultimately ends with this call to action, considering multiple avenues by which poverty might be combated, which by necessity would also entail the dismantling of these digital surveillance tools.

### Relate: Identify connections or links to other readings and scholarship
The main connection between this book and some of the other scholarship that I've encountered is that the automated decision making technologies and tools that Eubanks is describing are still designed in a way where they are performing predetermined goals that have been set in place by those system designers or programmers. With the development and rise of agentic AI technologies, however, as discussed by Acharya et al. (2025), Van Der Vlist et al. (2024), and Shariatpour & Behzadfar (2026), The risks associated with automated technologies and systems are only compounded by Agentic AI technologies because those technologies require less human oversight or intervention and are designed with the intention of performing tasks based on their own self directed goals. This could lead to a further entrenchment of techno solutionist logics that shift the blame away from the designers of these systems when they act in (un)intended ways onto the low income and marginalized communities that are the least likely to have their needs considered when such technologies are being developed .

## Intervention, Argument, Evidence
### Intervention: What is the intervention that the scholar is making, and where is that intervention being made? In other words, how do you place that intervention relative to existing disciplinary conversations that are taking place?
the opening and closing chapters of the book most strongly convey the intervention that Eubanks is making, Namely that The same kind of social divisions and power asymmetries that have existed between low income and impoverished communities and their middle class or wealthy counterparts has been maintained over the course of several hundred years in the United States. More specifically, she troubles arguments or a techno solutionist logics that frame automation as the solution to issues of class inequality, instead illustrating how automated and algorithmic technology is help to perpetuate and reify class divisions, a shift in orientation toward poverty that she refers to as the "digital poorhouse."

### Argument: What are the core argument(s) being made in the text?
Her ultimate argument then is to demonstrate how algorithms and automated technological systems perpetuate biases and discrimination against low income people, especially Black, brown, and Indigenous people of color.

### Evidence: What evidence is used to support the argument(s)?
She takes up three different automated and algorithmic systems that have been deployed as welfare management technologies across the United States within the last 10 to 15 years, including a system for managing health benefits in the Midwest, A coordinated entry system for housing placement of unhoused people in Los Angeles, and a risk assessment tool for predicting and preventing child abuse or neglect in Pennsylvania .
