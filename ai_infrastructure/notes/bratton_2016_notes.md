### Survey: Skim the text and identify main ideas
- Intro: Bratton starts off the book by making the argument for the affordances of the metaphor within hardware, software, and network development for the "stack" As a framework for understanding the structural organization of technological, geopolitical, Social, material, and digital infrastructures . He argues that understanding the architecture of planetary computing enables a more informed approach to design, especially one that is informed by the geopolitical formations in which this architecture has emerged and continues to evolve. He writes, " the project to be taken up sees politics as infrastructure, systems as law, totalities as site condition, supply chains as ecologies, and energy as money. Each of these, and each layer of The Stack drawn from them, is a unique but dependent logic of design and governance" (p. 15).

- Ch.1: In this chapter, Bratton outlines a political theory that informs his Understanding of the relationships between the various layers that comprise The Stack. Drawing on Carl Schmitt's _nomos_, Bratton considers how the drawing of concrete lines and borders as boundaries for nation states and the territory that they maintain sovereignty over Shifts with the development of capitalism and the rise of neoliberalism into a sovereignty that is bound not just to states, but to global markets, Especially in the context of globalization. he argues that this is important because the stack not only contributes to global geopolitical arrangements but is also a representation of those arrangements.

- Ch.2: He then offers a definition and explanation of platforms, which he describes as "references to _a standards-based technical-conomic system that simultaneously distributes interfaces through the remote coordination and centralizes their integrated control through that same coordination_" (p. 42). In other words, He argues that The Stack is itself a platform, or an architecture of many platforms , which operate as systems for pooling various kinds of things together into an organizational system that is both institutionalized and institutionalizes in turn. He offers several defining characteristics of platforms that can be employed in their analysis.

- Ch.3: In the next section of the book, Bratton offers an in depth explanation of each layer that makes up The Stack. he starts by considering the Earth, It's minerals and materials, the circulation of energy and resources, as a substrate from which computational systems are built.

- Ch. 4: He then looks at the layer of the Cloud, interrogating it as geopolitical space that transcends land alone, as technological corporations seize and circulate user data for their own sovereign purposes. He looks to examples or case studies regarding the cloud systems of companies such as Google or Apple.

- Ch. 5: Bratton considers the city to be a layer within The Stack that everyday users and their mobility in urban spaces To the layer of the cloud. Especially in the context of smart city models, Bratton looks at conflicts and sovereignty between the governmental or political institutions that govern urban municipalities in tension with the geopolitical forces that operate the cloud .

- Ch. 6: He then considers how information tying individual users and things are linked to the cloud through addresses, Which enable tracking, classification, and technological surveillance.
 
- Ch. 7: Furthermore, he considers how the interface layer offers a bridge between individual users and the numerous other layers of the stack, whether they be the earth layer, the cloud layer, the city layer, or the address layer. In other words, this chapter is especially concerned with how interfaces act as a mediator between these people and things and these other layers that they are engaging with , which thus enables user intervention and input into these layers through those mediating interfaces.

- Ch. 8: In alignment with post human or new materialist or other scholarship that seeks to trouble our understandings of what counts as human or agentic, Bratton argues that "_User_ Is a position not only through which we see The Stack, but also through which The Stack sees us, and so the platform sovereignities that it is most disposed to support are those that align with how the other layers organize their own intersecting interfacial regimes. The _Earth_ layer spins out polities of the electron and emergency, the _Cloud_ layer enrolls proto-citizens and global platform totalities, the _City_ chapter maps out spaces of filtering control and accidental illegal access, the _Address_ layer Discloses a landscape and reserve filled with things and events available for interaction,  and the _Interface_ layer diagram's reductive images of all of these processes, served up as total or tactical instrumental regimes. All these are put in motion for _Users,_ as _Users_ are put in motion for them" (p. 256).

- Ch. 9: In this chapter, Bratton Considers what is revealed by this framework for the Stack that is relevant for scholarship on design, especially in designing for new architectural formulations of _The Stack_ in the future. Essentially, he explains how a theoretical and political understanding of The Stack enables a speculative design for a new kind of planetary computing.

#### Part I: The Models

##### Introduction
In this introduction, Bratton begins by laying out the major crux of his argument that this book is centered around, Namely exploring the ways in which planetary scale computation has led to the emergence of a system where previous models for geopolitics or political geography no longer fit. He explains,

    I propose The Stack As a way that we might map political geography, But also for how we might understand the technologies that are making that geography. Planetary-scale computation takes different forms at different scales--energy and mineral sourcing and grids; subterranean cloud infrastructure; urban software and public service privitization; massive universal addressing systems; interfaces drawn by the augmentation of the hand, of the eye, or dissolved into objects; users both over-outlined by self-quantification and also exploded by the arrival of legions of sensors, algorithms, and robots. Instead of seeing all of these as a hodgepodge of different species of computing, spinning out on their own at different scales and tempos, we should see them as forming a coherent and interdependent whole (pp. 4-5).

In other words, The emergence of global technologies has meant that political geography has to be understood not only in terms of its relationship to the land and physical territories being napped and bordered for the purpose of nation states, which he describes as a Westphalian model , But also that technologies themselves are operating on a scale that they themselves become state like. He describes the stack as an accidental megastructure, A machine as state that emerges organically. He argues that his methodological commitment is to start with understanding planetary scale computation and develop a model for the way that computation is organized, and he starts there because he's arguing that in order to understand contemporary governance on a global scale, you have to understand the technologies that doing some of that governing work. He makes use of the metaphor of the stack that is often employed in network, hardware, and software design as a structuring model for understanding planetary scale computation. This model allows for an understanding of platforms, as essentially another unit for understanding geopolitics. In other words, he is recognizing that nation states are themselves geopolitical units that operate in relation to one another, But he is also stating that the stack itself allows for an understanding of how technology itself operates in a state like manner.

Bratton here is alluding to a problem of conventional understandings of political geography, as even within operating the typical nation state model that is bound to land boundaries is itself complicated by the fact that there can be disputes over which nation state actually owns a given piece of land, or there can be claims beyond even the actual domain of land territories. I can think of a few examples of more traditional land disputes, such as disputes over the territorial ownership of Kashmir, or uncertainty over which nation has the jurisdiction over the waterways in the Strait of Hormuz. 

However, Bratton is acknowledging that there are limitations to a merely land bounded understanding of political geography, and he alludes to the idea that this is only more complicated when attempting to understand geopolitics in terms of planetary scale computation. For example, problems posed by extraterritorial corporate infrastructure on the part of major technological corporations that extends beyond the bounds of any finite nation states. In other words, He is acknowledging a limitation in contemporary understandings of political geography and is thus identifying a gap that his scholarship might be able to fill, Though this theoretical attempt will be just as speculative and slippery as the problem he is beginning with, due to the nature of geopolitics.

He makes the argument that the stack is an accidental megastructure, an organic and dynamic emergent machine that operates itself as a state, but not one that has been predetermined or organized in advance according to intentional planning and design. He highlights several examples of how the lines between technology and state power have become blurred, as has been the case for legal or legislative battles between major technological corporations with the legislative authority of nation states. He argues that this presents a new problem for everyday people, as they are now tasked with grappling with their status as citizens of the nation states whose territories in which they reside, but also as users within technological ecosystems that extend beyond the traditional boundaries of nation states. Rather than argue that these kinds of contradictions or tensions that people are faced with are exceptions to our understanding of political geography, he instead argues that the stack is a way for understanding this new geopolitical order, one that can be understood with this model of The Stack and its constituent layers (i.e. Earth, cloud, city, address, interface, user). He further highlights that each of these layers themselves had to be understood as geopolitically complex-- the cloud infrastructure for a given technology may stretch across borders, a user may be a refugee or dual-citizen of two states with competing national interests, the location address may be obscured for its territorial jurisdiction through the use of a VPN, etc.

He starts to consider more closely what kinds of theoretical problems are posed by the geopolitical complexities raised in the computational era, which are apparent in the several examples I just raised (i.e. extraterritorial cloud infrastructure, VPNs, etc.). He explains that this Raises questions as to our understanding of sovereignty, And now Nation states must task themselves with confronting the problem of planetary scale computation if they are to maintain their power and sovereignty. Similar to his earlier acknowledgement that the theoretical framework that he is proposing is speculative, Bratton argues that to understand the many connections between the layers of the stack requires that one understands movements between layers as a blur, a mapping that is too rapidly moving and emergent to definitively pinpoint. Just as The Stack acts as an accidental megastructure That is emerging out of the meeting of all of these different layers or meshes of people, material, and things that enable global computing, A recognition of the moving connections between all of these layers and things is necessary if we are to imagine a design otherwise.

##### The _Nomos_ of the Cloud
He starts off with Carl Schmitt's notion of nomos, Or the act of territorial drawing that enables occupation and claims to a territory, which he sees as the necessary act in order for any kind of sovereignty over land to be enacted. Bratton, extends sovereignty to the realm of markets and platforms, however, arguing that markets themselves move beyond territoriality of land to the territory of economic exchange, and platforms to infrastructure and political subjects. In other words, the territorial lines that historically served as the land-based boundaries for nation-states are distinct from the comparatively much more contested and mobile lines of computationally based jurisdiction.

To continue to build on this idea of the line, Bratton points to several instances in which boundary marking was demonstrably unclear, such as in his example of a photo of a Bosnian concentration camp photo, where the photographer contested whether the photo showed his position as outside the camp's fencing (i.e. him in a position of power) or, as he argues, within the fencing (i.e. being subject to incarceration). He argues that this is just one example of how line drawing or borders can have various geopolitical effects depending on how they are drawn or perceived or are placed in position relative to subjects. He states that a land based topology for boundary marking of nation states is a normative loop topology rooted in a Schmittian understanding of nomos, whereas The Stack operates on a different, multiple topology. He digs further into Schmittian's nomos, which Schmitt identified as land specific, a geopolitical theory that Bratton contends is incompatible with the propensities of The Stack to extend beyond land, air, and sea, evident when we consider the issue of satellites and undersea cables as the cloud-based infrastructure that network communication is reliant on, for example.

The sixth section illustrates how technology itself can physically mark boundaries (i.e. the use of network cables as both information transit and physical grid marking) and also collapses the Schmittian distinctions between air, land, and see (as is the case with undersea cables that are connected to land-based network cable infrastructure). Just as land-sea-air collapse, drawing on scholarship from Fredric Jameson, Bratton argues that the division between the physical and the digital or virtual collapse.

The seventh section focuses more closely on the Großraum, a geopolitical order rooted in larger spheres of influence that allowed for multipolar configurations of organize state interests (i.e. the Western bloc in opposition to the Soviet bloc in the mid-20th century). Schmitt Looks to the Monroe Doctrine as a positive example For how a nation state might claim their territorial sovereign, But with the emergence of international law and organizations such as the United Nations, a clear-cut sense of what constitutes the interior and exterior of a boundary, and its reversibility by extension, begins to collapse.

Likewise, the bridging together of the physical and the digital or the virtual that is a necessary feature of The Stack further collapses the digital, land, air, and sea into one, muddling or blurring the territorial distinctions that are characteristic of both Schmitt's nomos and early interest in Großraum.

Google acts as a representative example of how The Stack facilitates a new Großraum that is not bound merely to land-based territoriality, instead operating through its own technological sphere of influence, as itself a state, beyond the lines of nation-states.

##### Platform and Stack, Model and Machine
This chapter largely focuses on defining platforms in their primary characteristics, especially as The Stack operates as its own kind of platform. Here, Bratton defines a platform as a "_standards-based technical-economic system that simultaneously distributes interfaces through their remte coordination and centralizes their integrated control through that same coordination_" (p.42). In other words, platforms are a means by which to organize a system . Importantly, although platforms defined by The instructions that shape their design, their role in mediating action, and their political architecture, They cannot wholly be considered as predetermined plans. Instead, as a result of their organized nature, they can facilitate unplanned actions or uses, hence the accidental nature of The Stack as a megastructure. Here, platforms are designed with the intentions of aligning with a particular political program, and they rely on processes of standardization in order to enable the kind of mass scale organization or aggregation of things under the purview of a platform that may enable the furthering of the goals of a political program.

Platforms thus work through simultaneous centralization and decentralization, Acting as the infrastructural center or site that enables the interoperability of its constituent parts, centralizing some things while pushing others to the margins of its operation. Bratton outlines various key characteristics of a theory of platforms:
    - Platforms are the infrastructural site for action to occur
    - They both organize things through their standardization and enable idiosyncratic use on the part of the platform's users
    - Standardization is the basis for a principle of generative entrenchment, in which users are more likely to opt into platforms that standardize in ways that increase their interoperability and render it easier for users to "plug into" the standards of a given system
    - Users operate within the constraints of a platform to reprogram its components
    - Platforms require the use of formal models to facilitate this standardization and ability to integrate its various components
    - Users may gain a surplus in value for information that is mediated by a platform, while the platform itself is made up of the value of all user-inputted information
    - Platforms centralize in that they are the means by which information is aggregated, but that does not mean that they are themselves a central actor
    - Platforms, through their status as a mediating structure, generate identities for the users who are connected to it
    - Users are not governed equally or symmetrically by platforms
    - Ideal platforms are invisible, maintaining user focus on the information and actions that it mediates, rather than its role as the mediating infrastructure
    - Components are interchangeable, hence the emphasis on the interoperability of platforms
    - Platforms govern both in users' instantaneous interactions and also cumulatively through platform-user interactions over time
    - Ideal platforms are able to not only interact with users on the basis of their programmatic design, but are also able to anticipate their future design on the basis of these interactions
    - Platforms that effectively organizing existing heterogenous information achieve generative entrenchment more quickly
    - Platforms link a variety of actors/actants across multiple spatiotemporal scales simultaneously
    - Platforms are opaque-- they don't look like how they work or work how they look
    - The sovereignty of platforms is not a given- this sovereignty depends on what/how a platform mediates in relation to other existing social, cultural, technical, and economic institutions

A Stack is a platform, and _The Stack_ is a platform that extends across the Earth, into the depths of its waters and the air and space encompassing it, hence its important function as an aggregate that enables planetary scale computation. It is a system that organizes many systems as its components, empowered by the interoperability of these multifaceted systems when pulled together.

The Stack as a political machine emerged out of the social and communication structures of its designers, a principle described in Conway's law. Likewise, the Stack operates as a political machine as it shapes global social, economic, and communication structures in turn.

To illustrate how The Stack is both designed or program on the basic of social and communicative values and also an active designer of those values in turn, Bratton considers two examples of Stack designs, including Beer's design of a stack for the Chilean government that sought to bring forth a new sociopolitical arrangement, which stands in contrast to Sakamura's design for a stack in Japan that would only further entrench existing sociocultural values by enabling better distribution of infrastructural tasks across different sectors in ways that still maintain interoperability with the larger system

Bratton states that the internet is much like these exemplary stacks, faciliting user information exchange. He points out how the TCP/ICP network model was able to bring together previously incompatible systems, which, based on the principle of generative entrenchment, enabled easy user buy-in to this aggregated information highway system.

He then discusses each layer of the stack, the Earth, cloud, city, address, interface, and user, as heterogenous elements that, through their dynamic connections, illustrate the interoperability and sheer scale of The Stack. He outlines the key focus of each layer of the stack, and how he will discuss each layer in Part II of the book.

#### Part II: The Layers

##### Earth Layer
He starts off by distinguishing between computation and computers, The former of which is more ontological in nature , and the latter of which involves the discovery of computers on the part of humans and the development of machines to perform computational work. He critiques the idea of what he calls a social Turingism, or the idea that computers will become developed enough to match the capacities of an ontology of computation such that technological progress toward this goal is economically beneficial and desirable. In other words, he writes, "it is important to distinguish the limits of formal computation, on the one hand, from what the limits of actual computational technologies can really do, on the other" (p. 79).

Importantly, in an attempt to develop and proliferate the machines that will never fully achieve the full scope of computation that they seek to perform in name only, The earthlier makes up all of the materials from which these machines are made . Here, the stack acts as an insatiable Mechanical pit, extracting from and reshaping the Earth in its ongoing consumption.

It is from this dual process of ongoing consumption and dispensing of waste upon the Earth that leads Bratton to consider the geoaesthetics of the Stack, or its ability to shape the Earth in its own image simultaneous to the Earth shaping the image of the Stack.

In order to shape the earth in its own image, however, ncecessarily requires that The Stack render the Earth visible. In doing so, it looks for what is visible on the surface, taking what is visible as the information to be captured in this layer of The Stack. He considers the use of Very Long Baseline Interferometry (VLBI) as the infrastructural visual machine through which telescopes are positioned outward into space, the inverse of which might be seen in the use of satellites to map the face of the Earth for the purpose of Google Earth. Importantly, this mapping is itself is informed by a logic that renders some things visible and others invisible. What _can_ be seen visibly, as a result of its monitoring, be modified or changed.

    Computation is training governance to see the world as it does and to be blind like it is. If, over time, something sees for the state, on behalf of the state and in place of the state, it does so by seeing as a state, or by seeing as something the state has not yet become but would become once it's trained by these same new tools of perception and blindness. as the state involves new techniques into itself, those techniques also absorb, displace, and diminish the state by controlling access to unique jurisdictions the state cannot otherwise possibly comprehend without their help. While it extends jurisdictionality, The Stack also confiscates and multiplies it. It doesn't merely accelerate or open up governance as currently configured; it invents substitutions as alternate jurisdictions appear, linking cellular biology to computational geopolitics, some enduring for seconds, others perhaps for many centuries.

What is captured in the vision of The Stack is part of what makes it operable as a model, relying on the continual collection of aggregated data to identify outliers and make predictions.

Here, however, The Stack acts as an ouroboros, a self-eating snake that requires the extraction of Earthly resources to facilitate the continued collection of information. This poses ecological problems, however, as Bratton questions how we might develop a Stack that is not outpaced by its own extractive hunger. In other words, how do we contend with the energy capture and use of The Stack and the scale of the computation it performs?

To be able to sense, to collect information through this vision, then, is what grants sovereignty. In Bratton's words: "the state 'takes' the territory that it is capable of sensing, as the data generate and guarantee a sovereign claim as much as (in this case, mre than) the sovereign claim guarantees the right to install a sensor and capture data" (p. 97). At a time in which ecological collapse and global emergencies become more frequent, due to the overconsumption of The Stack and intensifying consequences of anthropogenic climate change, there comes an increasing need to consider what is or is not rendered visible by the Stack and how it is designed relative to these emergencies.

Bratton thus distinguishes between two emergency design philosophies for The Stack: (1) designing _for_ emergency, which risks normalizing the circumstances of emergency and putting in place responses that render emergency circumstances more permanent, or (2) designing _with_ emergency, in which "...we engineer the path for one world to strategically fall apart into another" (p. 104).

He ends with final reflections on the relationship between vision, what is captured or organized under the purview of The Stack, and claims to sovereignty, with these main points:
    - "Determining what is and is not accounted for--addressed as being inside or outside the economy--is also a determination of what that sovereignty is accountable to" (p. 105)
    - "It arranges for both an absolutized surveillance, as well as the right not to see what appears in the lens, and so in this regard, 'seeing like a state' or 'seeing like a market' also means protecting blind spots as needed" (p. 106)

##### Cloud Layer
This chapter starts out with an explanation of how the cloud layer sits between both the earth layer and the city layer, And it is described as geopolitically unique in that multiple stakeholders or actors with their own cloud infrastructure my overlap in conflicting or contentious ways. Here, He cites Tor users who are operating on top of Amazon Web Services as one such example where competing interests, with distinct political ontologies, exist at the same infrastructural site. What is particularly unique about the cloud layer is that it is the site that grants access to other layers of the stack; It acts as a permeable layer, a filter for what is transmitted up to higher order layers from the computational work of the earth layer and back down again. Bratton writes, "as a governing nexus of The Stack, this order identifies, produces, and polices the information that can move up and down, layer to layer, fixing internal and external borders and designating passages to and from. In doing so, it generates more lines and borders, not fewer, and so its apparent universality is actually densely divided against itself" (p. 111).

This layer comprises not only the hardware that supports the software that the stack is dependent on, but it can also include cloud platforms, several of which are covered in this chapter, Including Facebook, Apple, Amazon, and Google. 

The cloud layer is thus distinctly logistical, facilitating the movement of things, Whether they be physical objects like the products that are distributed bye Amazon fulfillment centers, or digital and information streams , Like the packets that flow between user devices and data centers. Again, these logistical cloud networks can overlap, the cloud layer's "topography is shaped by the multiplication and superimposition of layers of sovereign claims over the same site, person, and event" (p. 111). He sees these points of convergence or super imposition as the battlegrounds between various sovereign actors, exemplified by his analysis of the "First Sino-Google War of 2009," A major cyber attack and data breach on Google's platform infrastructure that was conducted in coordination with Chinese national leadership, Demonstrating tensions between more traditional understandings of geopolitical jurisdiction and sovereignty that is land based and the physical virtual hybrid sovereignty of platforms such as Google. The problems posed by Cloud Platforms on the part of nation states Our only further exacerbated by the nature of cloud infrastructure, described by Bratton as a terraforming project in which the globe is taken over and reshaped by the logic of the given platform's architecture. It is in this embedding, on top of the Earth layer, that a _cloud polis_ emerges, The formation of a political entity comprised of its users, characterized by "hybrid geographies, incomplete governmental apparatuses, awkward jurisdictions, new regimes of interfaciality, archaic imagined communities, group allegiances, ad hoc patriotisms, and inviolable brand loyalties" (pp. 369-370). A cloud platforms establish their own protostate formations, however, it is their overlap or superimposition that fuels the competition for each of their geopolitical models. He thus identifies the key characteristics of the _cloud polis_ of Facebook, Apple, Amazon, and Google:

    - Facebook: "Facebook's social graph relies on at least four techniques: _identity and display, network closure, visual communication_ and _fabricated currencies_" (p. 126).
    - Apple: "The Apple model of _Cloud Polis_ is based on four primary emphases: _hardware/physical touch, App ecology, cult brand/leader,_ and _enclave aesthetics_ " (p. 129).
    - Amazon: "Amazon's central demands for that polity's prototypical future are _object logistics, retail compression and virtualization, procedural individuation,_ as well as _third-party hosting,_ the provision of wholesale _Cloud_ hosting at global scale" (p. 132).
    - Google: "For the Google platform model for the _Cloud Polis,_ these are all based on a grand vision encompassing (at least) _information cosmopolitanism, search, advertising, physicalized information,_ and _global infrastructure_" (p. 134).

##### City Layer
The city layer it's described in a manner that is most predominantly reminiscent of scholarship on the social production of space, or even media space (Couldry & McCarthy, 2003). He argues that the city layer is comprised of a "single, distributed urbanism" (p. 369) That stretches across the entirety of the planet , containing within it a variety of urban nodes or centers, or perhaps even urban enclaves. He especially is interested by the way that cities act both as simultaneous enclosures or enclaves and routes of passage or entry, comparing them to airports as a metaphor for how mobility and immobility can exist in a singular space at the same time, So the degree of access to freedom of movement is not equally accessible to all users. He explains that the city, as the spatial site where users encounter the stack through the interface layer, Begins to more closely mirror the social formation of what Deleuze describes as societies of control, where power becomes decentralized in its social imposition. 

What I found most useful or compelling in this chapter, however, was his discussion of parametricism and the development of corporate or company towns, and the impact of both on contemporary Urban Development. Given that the earth layer is the site where the possibilities of sensing for the purpose of extraction then enables the computational work of the cloud layer and the rise of platforms with their own polities and forms of sovereignty, It seems especially compelling to me that contemporary urban planning and management is so predominantly characterized by an interest in standardization, measurement, data collection, and the use of Quantitative analysis as the justificatory logic for future planning decisions. It explains the recursive practice employed in a state's vision (Scott, 1998), and the consequences of what a state fails to see and account for.

##### Address Layer
The address layer is described as the site where things in the world are enabled to participating in the transmission and reception of information or communication by way of their assignment to an address that is matchable to a resolver, a kind of standardized access that organizes the location data for any given thing. I found this chapter really interesting because he talks not only about the ideological and symbolic or Semantic choices that inform the classificatory or organizing logics That shape addressing practices, But he also talks about how addressability enables the collection of meta data, based on the relational nature between the user that is searching and the address that is being searched for. Furthermore, He explains that the scale of planetary computation is such that not only can metadata be collected about the point of search and the thing that is being searched, but there can be searches about searches, forming more complex relational layers the form of what he calls meta metadata. He makes use of several existing addressing Practices or standards to make some of these points, such as in discussion of how physical addresses are organized differently in various global context, And he also talks about some of the differences between IPV 4 and IPV 6 protocols , as the former of which is 32 bit and the latter of which is 128 bit, Both of which carry their own affordances and limitations in the addressable capacities of the Internet of Things. As was the case with is discussion of other layers of the stack in earlier chapters, however, he also discusses potential future implications for Design of the address layer . As further investment and developing of various nanotechnologies and biosensors continues, He asks what might come when it is computationally possible to address things at the level of cells. Overall, I found this chapter useful because he starts to give more of a tangible sense of how the stack is computational in that it is seeking to track and measure things, in the site of address is a very clear example of how something is rendered computationally readable or trackable.

##### Interface Layer
He then moves on to discuss how the interface layer serves as the site of mediation between the user and all of the other layers of the stack. It explains that interfaces are, by nature, these mediators that enable users to both affect and be affected by things in the world to which the interface serves as some sort of relay or control device. Of course, he talks about the visual nature of guys and the way that everyday users become enculturated to the use of visual interfaces, Such that the digital and physical divide becomes flattened, but he also talks about how interfaces are deployed and operated in such ways that they themselves can beget further interfaces. At one point, he makes a comment about how technology turtles all the way down, creating an aggregate or complex layered and hierarchical system of interfaces upon interfaces that then are not apparently visible or traceable to users, who then ironically deploy interfaces for the purpose of attempting to make these complex hierarchies visible through the use of data visualizations and so on. He describes multiple aspects of the infrastructure layer, and he notably places a lot of emphasis on the use of augmented reality tools as a very specific kind of interface in which users are overlaying digital and visual or symbolic information over top their perception of the immediate physical world. Here, He emphasizes that it is not only that AR enables this overlay of digital or virtual information on top of the physical, But the fact that a well designed AR interface is so naturalized that our user can feel the immediacy by which they allow the digital to affect and be affected by the world, in a very clearly ontological way.

##### User Layer
In his discussion of the user layer, described by Bratton as "not where the rest of the layers are mastered by some sovereign consciousness; It is merely where their effects are coherently personified" (p. 253). In other words, the user layer comprises the Actors or agents That embody or enact the computational effects of the stack at large .

#### Part III: The Projects

##### The Stack to Come
He then considers the relationship between the current existing stack and the potential stack or stacks that may unfold in the future, posing the relationship between these two states of the stack as a design problem. He essentially summarizes his discussion of each layer of the stack, and the mini stacks that comprise this totality of planetary computation , considering how this totality and its constituents stack simultaneously can be reimagined or confronted through design.

##### The Black Stack
Building on the discussion of the previous chapter, however, Bratton emphasizes that the stack that is to come is not fully visible to us, Potentially a result of our hurtling toward a post-human (as opposed to posthuman) world brought forth by anthropogenic climate change as enacted by the Stack. He argues that we have a limited sense of the pending nature of the stack to come, And we certainly see ourselves as implicated in bringing forward this future stack , but what is to ultimately come is not entirely Within our ability to fully grasp.
