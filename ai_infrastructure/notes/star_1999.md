### Survey: Skim the text and identify main ideas
- As a seminal text in critical infrastructure studies, Star makes a "call to study boring things" (p. 377), arguing that the design of the systems that underlie infrastructure Allow us to consider the ontological questions of these design choices , a matter that is especially important given An invisible nature infrastructure as supportive substrates from which other kinds of work emerge.

- Drawing on an earlier study that she wrote with Ruhleder, she provides several dimensions of infrastructure (pp. 381-382), including:
    - Its embeddness
    - Its transparency
    - Its extensive research or scope
    - Its function as knowledge to be acquired by the members of an infrastructural community of practice
    - Its links to the conventions of practice shared by an infrastructural community
    - Its embodiment of standards
    - Its establishment on an installed base
    - Its visibility in moments of breakdown
    - Its fixing in modular increments, not at once nor globally

- She argues that an ethnographic approach to the study of infrastructure enables analysis of the relational manner in which infrastructures are established, maintained, and are made sites of work

### Recite: Consider what you want to remember, and put that into your own words
The main purpose of this article is to both highlight the value of studying infrastructure while also providing guidance or lessons learned from Star's own experience doing ethnographic work to study infrastructural processes . She explains that Explicit attention to infrastructure helps to reveal power dynamics and social relations that underpin decision making processes for the formation of infrastructure, as well as inform the manner in which people perform actions as they operate within or as part of infrastructure. Based on her experience studying what she recognizes as previously invisible or understudied, she argues that infrastructure studies enabled for her "a more ecological understanding of workplaces, materiality, and interaction, and underpinned a social justice agenda by valorizing previously neglected people and things" (p. 379).

She offers several "tricks of the trade" (p. 384) for conducting the kind of ethnomethodological work that helps to reveal otherwise invisible aspects of infrastructure:

    1. She suggests that researchers should interrogate "master narratives," or the "voice that speaks unconsciously from the presumed center of things" (p. 384). In other words, She explains that researchers should be willing to question the naturalized or hegemonic categories, standards, and practices that are encoded into infrastructure. For example, This could mean interrogating the design of medical intake forms that include only two options for sex (i.e. male and female), which thus exclude the existence and experience of intersex individuals.

    2. Researchers should also be mindful of how and what they render visible in their analysis, as the decision to leave any work tacit or visible carries its own kind of risks and benefits that are contextually bound.

    3. Researchers should be mindful of the paradoxical nature of infrastructure, in which users may perform what appears to be extra work in order to avoid what they perceive as an obstacle. Here, researchers should look to both the "real-time adjustments, or _articulation_ work" (p. 385) that is performed alongside their typical productive or cooperative tasks.
