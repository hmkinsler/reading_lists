# Final Reflections
At the time of writing this note, it is the day before I sit my first written exam, and I have a couple of thoughts about what the process of preparing for the exams has been like. It might be the case that a future CRDM student stumbles across this repository and wonders why I created such a monstrous heap of code, annotations, random thoughts and notes, and evidence of the messiness of my preparation for the exams. The fact of the matter is that going into the exams process, I didn't really know what to expect or what it would feel like to attempt to carve out areas of scholarly expertise for myself, especially being an interdisciplinary scholar and researcher in an interdisciplinary program. In the course of all of the coursework that I completed for my graduate education, I found myself constantly captivated by a multitude of subjects, whether they pertained to computational approaches to studying media and language or materialist analysis or various rabbit holes that I jumped down in terms of computer science and engineering of technical systems and their design. But ultimately, I found that with the help of my committee members, I was able to find a way to embody my interdisciplinarity across these three lists, even as I also tried to hone in on the specific areas that I wanted to devote the time and training and skill to develop the necessary level of technical expertise to carry forward with me as part of the theoretical and methodological frameworks that will shape my dissertation and future scholarly work.

So, there are a few notes here that I would like to leave for anybody who may come across them, especially a future PhD student who may be just in their first year of their program who's trying to find their way and begin to carve out their scholarly identity. Or a student who is in the midst of putting together their own reading list and is wondering what should be included and what it's okay to leave out. Or even another student who is in the same position that I am right now, where it's the day before their exams, and they're wondering if they've truly committed themselves enough to engaging with all of their assigned material and whether they have absorbed that enough to contribute to those scholarly discussions and will be ready to move on to the next stage of their graduate career.

The first lesson I will share is that I have been surprised by my own intuition in formulating the kinds of texts that were present in each of my reading lists. Members of my committee can attest to the fact that I agonized over the creation of these lists because I especially worried that they would not be representative enough of the kinds of scholars and frameworks and approaches that I would be expected to know, especially when I enter the job market. Because I have had a somewhat non-traditional passage through my undergraduate career, when I entered graduate school, I often felt a sense of insecurity about my knowledge of canonical texts and concepts in comparison to my peers. And this fear manifested in my inclination to attempt to account for everything that I had not learned up until the point that I was developing these reading lists. So when it came time to write the rationale and include texts for the new materialism list, for example, I grappled with wanting to include what I saw as all of the big names in a given area. So I initially thought that I would need to include entire books from Bruno Latour and John Law and Michel Callon, and that I would need to bring in readings about power and other forces that shape our experience of the material world, including all of the canonical texts from Michel Foucault. This inclination of mine was pushed back on rightfully by my committee members, who assured me that the challenge here was not to cover the breadth of all knowledge that could be relevant for my research, but instead to really consider where I wanted to stake my claim and focus on the issues that are of particular relevance to my own work. I must admit that although I trusted my committee members then, and I know that what they shared with me is true now that I've completed these readings and am looking back and reflecting, at the time, I simply did not trust or believe them because I felt uncertain as to how I could trust my own expertise when there are just so many things that I have yet to have learned and encountered. Before I worked through my reading lists, I saw them and the exam process as the end of my research and scholarly career, at least as it pertains to exploring all of the literature and ideas and methods and frameworks that are available to me. Now that I have finished these readings, I think the most important lesson that I've learned is that these readings are instead the beginning of that work. They were not the end at all. Although I was really uncertain when I was putting the reading list together as to whether or not the texts that I selected were truly in an appropriate fit, given that I was skimming through them and attempting to make a very high-level assessment of how everything would fit together, what I've learned is that, in part, the function of the reading list is to identify the shape of the readings in relationship to each other on your own and to formulate your organization of those readings and their scholarly and disciplinary traditions through your own reading of them. And that to be able to do so, to organize the readings in various groups and to identify the ways in which they converge and diverge on a number of issues that emerge through your reading is itself a sign of readiness for the exams. So, one piece of advice that I would provide to myself if I were able to go back in time, I would tell my past self that part of what I was looking for when I first formulated the list was precisely the kind of knowledge that would emerge from the act of reading. The piece of advice I would give would be to trust your instinct more and allow your intuition to lead you into the kind of reading of the texts on your lists that helps you make sense of the shape of your given area and stake your claim in it as a developing scholar and researcher.

I must also say, quite candidly, that my sense of myself as a writer has changed in several ways over the course of preparing for the exams. If you asked me what kind of a writer I was a year ago, I would almost certainly say that I didn't see myself as much of a writer at all, and that I consider the act of textual presentation of my thoughts to be the area where I am weakest as a scholar. One of my goals going into preparing for the exams then was to really develop and work on this skill that I saw as a meaningful shortfall of my abilities to present my ideas to public-facing audiences. This repository, in many ways, is evidence of all of the practice that has gone into becoming a more confident writer and attempting to find my voice in the midst of many other voices that I was reading from. I've included a note as part of this Read Me document that I began to employ speech-to-text and dictation tools in an attempt to experiment with what scholarly writing could look like for me processually. I experimented with many a speech-to-text tool, including the native accessibility capabilities and functions for both Windows 10 and 11, the latter of which is known as Microsoft Windows voice access feature. There was a great deal that was left to be desired when it came to my use of each of these tools. In some cases, they were not able to properly navigate grammatical norms and stylistic and formatting issues pertaining to punctuation and the like. I also attempted to make use of various models that are present in VS Code, where I did the vast majority of my writing through markdown files. I also made use of the speech-to-text feature that is natively integrated in Google Docs text editor. I attempted to use the Windows dictation tool, and I finally ended up making use of Handy, an open source tool that enables installation of local models that are trained on transcribing acoustic data. At the time that I am writing right now, I'm using Parakeet TDT 0.6B V2 as the transcription model that helps make my thinking legible as written text on a screen or page. 

It has been challenging to adjust to this mode of writing because in many ways it is quite different than typing or writing by hand, but it is a mode of writing that I am very grateful that I experimented with. It ultimately revealed to me a lot of interesting issues when it pertains to accessibility measures and the way that we navigate our own identity and processes for working in the kinds of logics and mode of production that is expected in the context of academic and scholarly institutions. There were times where I felt frustrated by how challenging it was to make effective use of these accessibility tools, which I found ironic given the audience that they are often intended for. I identified several issues with the integration of these speech-to-text tools for VS Code, for example, including in the types of editor modes that are available and whether or not they allow API integration, which for whatever reason was interfering with my ability to use dictation tools. Ultimately, however, if I were to revisit the question of whether or not I see myself as a writer, I think I would push back potentially and draw on the call from Shipka (2011), who is present in my critical making list, to move _Toward a composition made whole_ and ask not just what it means to write in a textual mode and what it means to develop a sense of one's professional and scholarly identity as a writer based on that perspective, but instead to ask whether or not I see myself as a composer. Here, I would say, is where I place myself. I see myself as a composer and that I have to grapple with the challenges of the technical systems with which I interface as part of my composition process. Not only then is my ability to write shaped by my understanding of the content of the readings and my ability to articulate my own response to those readings and my ability to synthesize ideas and respond to them and apply them to my own examples and case studies, but it is also contingent on my ability to set up the very tools that I compose with, making sure that my microphone is working properly with the given text editor interface that I am connecting my own voice to. Whether or not I make use of some sort of semantic review of my own writing with the assistance of a large language model or not, and the kinds of critical decision-making that that requires and the technical knowledge and expertise that is invoked in my performance of the act of writing. I've learned a lot from this experiment in reframing what my writing process looks like, what it feels like, and what it ultimately results in as it makes my thinking and ideas legible to readers. I've always struggled to write at-length the kind of academic prose that I so value from the texts I engage, and when I completed a mock version of my written exams, I was surprised that I had written just around six pages in two hours-- something that I would have otherwise considered impossible. That transformation of my approach to my own writing has been well worth the challenge of the exams process.

Finally, then, the last reflection I would share has to do with the affective experience of working on preparing reading lists and drudging through all of the material and finally preparing for the written and oral exams. And what I would say is that this has been a process that has come with many highs and very lows for me, personally. There were times where I would catch myself as I was writing something in my notes or posing a question or writing an annotation in the margins of a reading, and I would feel like I had stumbled onto an idea, some kind of _eureka!_ moment, where I would find myself reminded of what it is that I so love about the world that is scholarly practice. There have, at other times, been lows where my own imposter syndrome has crept in as I struggle to find my way through incredibly dense texts and materials that, upon a first or even second, or third pass, still did not feel transparent to me. Some notable mentions from my own list include Benjamin Bratton's (2016) _The Stack_, Deleuze and Guattari's (1986) _A Thousand Plateaus_ and Manuel Delanda's (1997) _A Thousand Years of Nonlinear History_. As I was reading through these texts, I found myself wondering whether or not it was truly possible that I would pass the exams because I felt as though I simply could not grasp fully the argument of these scholars and their significance. And I spent what I would describe as the majority of the time reading, thinking that I was not properly engaging with the material or understanding it. It was not until about two weeks ago that as I started making my full pass of the entirety of the readings for each list and all of their materials and started to organize them and draw connections across the readings as opposed to deeply approaching each of them in isolation that I discovered that I was beginning to read the texts differently. And I think there is where I started to see what is the transformational nature of the reading lists and what it is that is valuable about engaging in this process. And I think that I'm coming out on the other side of that with a lot more questions and a sense of what the task is at hand for preparing to dissertate and set forth on the next stage of what I hope will be a very long and fulfilling and productive career, drawing on the very kinds of perspectives and skills that were conveyed to me through these texts. This kind of undertaking can be exhausting and demoralizing and downright anxiety inducing at times. I cannot lie that I am pretty terrified to take my written exams tomorrow and throughout this week and I don't feel ready by any means. But I think ultimately what ends up happening is that you become so familiar with your own texts and you come to know them so well, better than anyone, that the more you engage with them, the more gaps that you see. The more times you'll think to yourself, "oh, I wish I had added this other reading here", or "I wish I had more time to engage with this example", or "I wish I had already done this kind of analysis", or "I wish I could spend more time writing about this subject before moving on." And it is once you reach that stage that you begin to realize that even if you don't feel ready, you are ultimately at the point where you have to make the leap of faith and complete the exams.

So it is here that I leave my final reflections on the whirlwind that has been the PhD qualifying exams process, with lots of new questions, some memories of late night reading, and after _many_ trips to the nearest cafe to grab a hot matcha latte to keep my hands warm as I flip through pages and pages of text in Ricks Annex. On to the next one!
