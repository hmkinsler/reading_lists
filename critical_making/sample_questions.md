# Sample Qualifying Exam Questions: Critical Making

These questions are designed to simulate the kind of broad synthetic prompts a committee might pose for a four-hour, 15-20 page qualifying exam essay drawing on the totality of the critical making reading list.

---

## Question 1 — Foundational Synthesis

Ratto defines critical making as a practice that bridges conceptual and material engagement with technology, offering a three-part framework that moves from literature review through prototyping to critical reflection. Drawing on the full scope of your reading list, trace how subsequent scholars have extended, complicated, or challenged this foundational definition. In what ways has critical making as a scholarly practice evolved beyond Ratto's original formulation — particularly as scholars have attended to embodiment, relationality, and the politics of who makes and for whom — and what is at stake in how we continue to define and theorize it?

*What this tests:* Your ability to trace the intellectual genealogy of the list as a whole, moving from Ratto's foundational piece through crip technoscience, relational rhetorics, design justice, and decolonial frameworks without treating any one text as the final word.

---

## Question 2 — Embodiment, Situatedness, and Maker Identity

Haraway argues that all knowledge is situated — produced from a partial, embodied perspective that must be made accountable rather than concealed. Drawing on Haraway alongside Hamraie and Fritsch's crip technoscience manifesto, Cipolla's feminist maker pedagogy, and Elam and Wiley's analysis of subjectivation in the university makerspace, develop a theory of what it means to be a maker-scholar. How do embodied identities — including disability, gender, neurodivergence, and institutional position — shape both the process and the politics of making? What are the implications of such a theory for scholars who bring their own situatedness explicitly into the design and research process?

*What this tests:* Your ability to synthesize the maker-scholar identity strand of the list, while also accounting for the autistic and feminist dimensions of your own scholarly positionality as described in your rationale.

---

## Question 3 — The Politics of Design and Design Justice

Costanza-Chock argues that design reproduces the matrix of domination unless it actively works against it, while scholars such as Gollihue and Hamraie and Fritsch demonstrate that the bodies and power relations shaping makerspaces are rarely neutral. Drawing on at least five texts from your reading list, construct an argument about what it means to pursue design justice through critical making. In your response, address the tensions between techno-utopian or techno-solutionist discourses that often circulate in maker culture and the resistant, communal, and politically accountable making practices that scholars on your list propose as alternatives.

*What this tests:* Your ability to put design justice, crip technoscience, and critical making into productive tension, and to demonstrate why those tensions matter practically and theoretically.

---

## Question 4 — Decolonial Participatory Methods and the Ethics of Co-Design

A significant portion of your reading list grapples with what it means for researchers to engage in design and making with — rather than for — communities. Drawing on Escobar's designs for the pluriverse, Becerra's decolonial methodology, Itchuaqiyaq and Matheson's cautions about the metaphorization of decoloniality in TPC, Itchuaqiyaq et al.'s framework of cultural humility, Hayhurst's decolonial feminist digital PAR, and Winschiers-Theophilus et al.'s participatory design chapter, what methodological and ethical commitments distinguish genuinely decolonial critical making from liberal participatory design that merely incorporates community voices? How do scholars on your list navigate the persistent tensions between institutional research imperatives and the autonomy, local epistemologies, and sovereignty of the communities they work with?

*What this tests:* Your command of the decolonial strand of your list and your ability to articulate why the distinction between co-design, participatory design, and decolonial design is not merely semantic.

---

## Question 5 — Materiality, Ecology, and the Life of the Maker Text

Burgess traces the material and ecological life cycle of her Twitter bot, from rare earth minerals to data centers to carbon emissions, in order to ask what critical makers owe in their accounting of the technologies they build. Drawing on Burgess alongside Ratto's theory-technology mapping, Resch et al.'s account of thinking as handwork, Shipka's theory of multimodal composition, and Shivers-McNair's relational rhetorics, consider how this body of scholarship invites makers to account for both the material conditions of production and the rhetorical force of the maker texts that emerge from those processes. Where do the scholars on your list agree about what makers are responsible for attending to, where do they diverge, and what gaps remain?

*What this tests:* Your ability to synthesize the materialist and rhetorical strands of the list and speak to what the process of making produces beyond its immediate prototype.

---

## Question 6 — Grassroots, Institutional, and Community Contexts of Making

Elam and Wiley demonstrate that university makerspaces impose particular logics of subjectivation on makers, while Sipos and Åkerman's Critical Making Responsibility Framework and Sipos's chapter on grassroots critical making reveal how those institutional logics can conflict with the values of grassroots and community-based innovation. Drawing on these texts alongside others from your list, what are the conditions — spatial, institutional, epistemological, and relational — under which critical making can be a genuinely resistant or liberatory practice? And conversely, under what conditions does critical making reproduce the very power structures it claims to interrogate?

*What this tests:* Your ability to hold institutional critique and political possibility in tension across the full list, particularly the conversations between university-based scholarship and grassroots/community-embedded practice.

---

## Question 7 — Application to Your Own Research

Your critical making reading list is organized in part to ground your own work developing a DIY book scanner for the Fourth Ward Oral History Project in partnership with the Fourth Ward Historic Neighborhood Association. Drawing on the full scope of your reading list, construct an argument for what it means to develop archival technologies *in partnership with* a community whose history includes forced displacement, research extraction, and erasure from the historical record. In your response, address the specific methodological, ethical, and political commitments that this body of scholarship equips you to bring to that project — and the tensions or contradictions it reveals that you have not yet resolved.

*What this tests:* Your ability to apply the totality of the list to your own research context, demonstrate reflexivity about your positionality, and engage honestly with what remains difficult or unresolved. This is likely the question your committee finds most generative, as it connects the theoretical and the practical.

---

## Notes on Likely Committee Priorities

Given the explicit focus of the list's rationale on (1) embodied and situated making, (2) critical making as communal and resistant practice, and (3) grounding the Fourth Ward book scanner project in this scholarship, the questions most likely to appear are some version of Q2, Q4, or Q7 — or a hybrid that combines the theoretical with the applied. Q7 in particular is a classic qualifying exam move: it requires synthesis of the whole list while demonstrating that you know how to *use* that scholarship, not merely recite it.
