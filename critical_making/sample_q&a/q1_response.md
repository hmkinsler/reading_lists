### Intro and Thesis
Though the origins of critical making scholarship largely center on Matt Ratto's (2011) framework for making as a means to engage in the mediation of critical social and technological concepts through engagement with materials, scholarship has since more complexly considered the ontological and social implications that arise from making and design practices, whether due to the embodiedness of makers, the wicked problems that makers seek to explore in their practice, or the complex and locally situated needs of communities that are engaged in critical making research.
### Foundations in critical making scholarship
- Matt Ratto (2011) -- making as a means to perform critical conceptualization and theoretical work through engagement with technology and the material world
- Hertz et al.'s (2012) zine project begins to extend beyond the initial definition of critical making offered by Ratto (2011) to understand its implications in the context of the broader DIY and maker movement's development in the late 20th and early 21st centuries
### Embodied making, care, and repair
- Gollihue and Shivers-McNair take up original conceptualization of critical making to consider not just the critical conceptual work that making enables, but also the embodied, relational and situated nature in which that work takes place. Here, they see ontological implications for understanding the experience of makers in relation to the tools they employ (i.e. 3D printing, soldering, etc.), the materials they use, the spaces they inhabit, etc.
- Critically, they both also stress the importance of understanding making beyond just "technological" or digital forms, but also practices such as farming, sewing and textiles, etc.
### Critical making's role in decolonial participatory methods
- Escobar (2018) argues that design is ontological in that it enables worldbuilding, especially in affirmation of pluriversality, as opposed to the imposition of a single hegemonic modern onto-epistemology
- Decolonial participatory design embraces Escobar's ontological politics, suggesting that researchers enagge with local indigenous communities to work on design that imagines otherwise
- Critical making comes into play here because as Sipos (2025) points out, the term may not always align with local values, an issue that Itchuaqiyaq et al. warn to be mindful of, so it's worth exploring local communities understanding of design vs. making in relationship to criticality and world builing
### Convergences and divergences
- Converge: Decolonial methods uplift relationality and nondualism, drawing on theoretical scholarship that rose out of the ontological turn, especially from STS scholars like Karen Barad, Lucy Suchman, Bruno Latour, Tim Ingold, and Donna Haraway, who are cited frequently in both the work from Gollihue and Shivers-McNair to capture the non-dualist nature of makers' embodied experiences in relation to the spaces, places, materials, animals, and more they engage with, as well as in Escobar's rejection of the cartesian dualist approach within the dominant onto-epistemological tradition. In other words, decolonial participatory methods extend this nondualist ontology to consider the stakes of other onto-epistemologies in instances where researchers engage with communities as co-designers to confront societal issues posed by coloniality.
- Converge: The social, political, and cultural implications of technology are part of what's at stake for Ratto (2011), who argues that the criticality of making helps to reveal these contextual factors that shape the construction of technology. Similarly, decolonial participatory researchers are tasked with understanding the political and social risks of employing a given technology as part of their research methods, as Hayhurst mentions with the risks of using GoPro video methods in her study, and also as part of their maker practice, as Sipos mentions is an issue raised by local Indonesian communities who faced issues sourcing affordable microcontroller technologies to work with.
- Diverge: Ratto's framework comes across as more pedagogically focused in nature, perhaps drawing too much of a distinction between the conceptualization work that can be performed through material engagement as opposed to through written or oral and aural modes alone. Though this concern with materiality is shared by many of the scholars on this list, he fails to fully dig into the justification for why making is a distinct practice than making, an issue that Sipos highlights as the term may not always align with local values, an issue that Itchuaqiyaq et al. warn to be mindful of, so it's worth exploring local communities understanding of design vs. making in relationship to criticality and world building. This issue is even raised in the interview that Hertz has with Ratto in his 2012 compiled zine book project. Ultimately, there are variations in how criticality in relation to design vs. making is conceived differently across the readings.
