## Outline and Thesis
### Intro and Thesis
The book scanner takes up work in critical making in several ways: (1) explore design and making as ontological acts that have the capacity to respond to systemic oppression, as was the case for the erasure of historical records pertaining to the Fourth Ward, (2) understand what it means to join alongside a community as a researcher to identify problems that could be remedied through the development of a critically made text, (3) develop ethical means by which to bridge gaps between my own role operating within institutional spaces with the needs of a historically marginalized community of which I am not a part.
## The book scanner as a participatory design project
- The need for the book scanner as a solution for digitizing and archiving historical materials for the Fourth Ward Oral History Project only came after I became personally associated and connected with Fourth Ward residents, who I met organically out of our shared housing justice advocacy work in the City of Raleigh
- To successfully engage in decolonial participatory research for this project, I am informed by:
	- Itchuaqiyaq et al.'s guidance to take cultural humility, an understanding that one is coming in as an outsider with not pre-existing expectations, but instead a respect for the cultural norms, values, beliefs, and needs of the community
	- Sipos' description of the various contextual and local considerations that should shape maker practice in these communities (i.e. whether a project is ecologically sustainable, whether it conflicts with the community's beliefs, etc.)
	- Escobar's argument that design is ontological, adn the book scanner has the capacity to both do harm (if not developed in alignment with the community's needs and vision) and good (to uncover the records that depict the process by which urban renewal took place and make them publicvally available)

## Positionality and power for maker scholars
-  In the case of developing the book scanning project, I am being asked to leverage my institutional and technical / material expertise to help actually construct and develop the workflow for the community archive according to the community's goals-- I'm a kind of mediator there, so I was tasked with identifying an adequate scanning solution, which prompted me to work with the open-source hardware design I encountered
- My investment in DIY ethos is largely in alignment with that expressed by many scholars in the maker movement (Hertz et al.)
- This requires careful consideration of the limitations of my own situatedness and partial perspective (Haraway) as a researcher and maker, however, as I am both operating within the constraints of a Western-centric academic institution that is shaped by its modernist onto-epistemological stance (Escobar), as well as a white scholar who must take on the reflexive work required to ethically work with residents in this historically Black community. Here, embracing the connectedness of my own partial vision to those of the residents I work with (Harway) is essential, as is understanding the multiple, or pluriversal (Escobar), perspectives shared by all of us who are collaboratively working on the oral history project together.
