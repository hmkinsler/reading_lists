# Critical Making

## Rationale
This list takes up scholarship in critical making, which Ratto describes as “a mode of materially productive engagement that is intended to bridge the gap between creative physical and conceptual exploration” (2011, p. 252), or a practice employed by scholars to interrogate technology through embodied prototyping and creation of material texts. More specifically, this list seeks to explore what it means to envision making as an embodied, communal, and resistant practice that scholars and communities alike employ to resist the imposition of technologies that are often designed without their needs in mind in favor of centering the creative, culturally shaped, and local epistemologies that makers utilize as they envision alternative technological futures. To do so, the first two sections of the list explore foundational scholarship on critical making, considering how it has been defined and theorized as a method for not only developing critical technological literacy (e.g. Garnet Hertz, Matt Ratto) but also as a means to participate in collective care, repair, and confrontation of the sociopolitical and systemic issues raised by techno-utopianism. The next section explores what it means to be a maker-scholar, considering the implications of identifying oneself as a maker, while also accounting for the embodied experience of tinkering, working with materials, and developing creative projects as a scholarly practice. In particular, as an autistic scholar, this section allows me to consider how my own situatedness (Hamraie & Fritch, 2019; Haraway, 1991) plays a role in my own critical engagement with the technologies and materials that both shape my pedagogy (Cipolla, 2019) and scholarship. The following section then looks to research in participatory action and design that more broadly considers how critical making is one aspect of a broader collection of decolonial methods (Escobar, 2018) that are employed in the co-construction of knowledge with local communities.

Ultimately, this list seeks to ground myself in scholarship that captures the multifaceted nature of critical making as a scholarly practice and process, particularly as that process is embodied by makers themselves, informed and shaped by makers’ and communities’ epistemological and embodied perspectives, and culminates in a maker text that carries its own implications for the co-construction of research and knowledge as communities interrogate, respond to, reinvent, and reimagine technologies. As a means for bridging theory and methodological practice together, this body of scholarship will be essential in informing my own critical making work, particularly in the case of the book scanner project that I am developing for the Fourth Ward Oral History Project, which emerged out of conversation with former residents of the neighborhood who have established the Fourth Ward Historic Neighborhood Association as a 501(c)(3) non-profit focused on public education about the history of their neighborhood, raising awareness about the sociopolitical consequences of urban renewal projects during the mid 20th century, and reconnecting residents who were forcibly displaced from the neighborhood. Overall, I look to critical making to ask what it means to develop archival technologies in partnership with the community for whom that technology is intended to serve.
