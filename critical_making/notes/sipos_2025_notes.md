# Notes for In the field: Grassroots critical making (Sipos, R., 2025)

## Citation Information
Sipos, R. (2025). In the field: Grassroots critical making. In *Critical technical practice in grassroots innovation: Proposals of change through Indonesian collective intrinsic design* (pp. 113-219). Springer VS. https://link.springer.com/book/10.1007/978-3-658-49695-1

# Reading Notes
## SQR3
### Survey: Skim the text and identify main ideas
- This chapter focuses on presenting the findings of fieldwork conducted in this book, which was conducted with grassroots design and innovation practitioners in Indonesia

- Sipos opens by surveying core literature in critical making scholarship, most heavily from Garnet Hertz, and then argues that this scholarship needs to be extended to community based contexts where grassroots practitioners are engaging in design and making work in spaces typically not captured in critical making research

- She summarizes her experience with several maker workshops and conferences that ultimately affirmed Sipos' perspective that existing critical making frameworks were not sufficiently accounting for making work taking place outside of Western spaces

- She then presents the design of her field research in Java, Indonesia, where she:
    - Mapped out several makerspace and grassroots innovation communities in the area and engaged in field visits to those sites
    - Conducted a participatory action research project (PAR) with one of the maker communities at these sites
    - Due to the pandemic, she ultimately planned a hackathon with one of these communities

- Based on the observations of her field research, she argues that her data is representative of the pluriversal nature of making and innovation practices across this community, drawing on cultural knowledge variably in different spaces, including those that were more urban or rural. She additionally highlights the unique design challenges faced by practitioners in Indonesia, responding to problems resulting from the colonial history of their land. This is exemplified in her analysis of numerous projects that were mapped during her fieldwork.

- She then offers two case studies to focus her discussion and analysis on:
    - "This first case study investigates a West Javanese grassroots innovation collective that acts as a supporting network for Indonesian grassroots communities. It also engages in maintaining community-based internet infrastructure (better known as _community network_) projects and organizing rural ICT camps for skill sharing" (p. 154)
    - The second case study involves description of the _Art-Science Collective_, "a Central Javanese collective that has been at the forefront of critical making for decades. This first part aims to shed light on how the same collective can experiment with terraforming a volcano, contribute to space research, and organize a civic hackathon in a pandemic... This collective initiated the second  layer in this analysis, the intrinsic design case study of the #hackfromhome hackathon" (p. 182)

### Query: Write questions to consider as you read
- Similar to my question posed in response to Sipos & Åkerman (2023), how does the invoking of the term "innovation" come into potential tension with the decolonial perspective on design that is present throughout the piece, including in her use of Escobar's (2018) concept of pluriversality?

- Itchuaqiyaq & Matheson (2021) highlight a tendency for TPC scholars to employ "decolonial" as a metaphor for work that is more aptly described as "anticolonial," given the centrality of rematriation and explicit focus on Indigenous sovereignty over land that is described by the word "decolonial." Given this, how is the notion of a decolonial perspective to design and participatory action research employed or potentially troubled in Sipos' analysis?
