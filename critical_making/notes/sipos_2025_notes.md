Sipos, R. (2025). In the field: Grassroots critical making. In *Critical technical practice in grassroots innovation: Proposals of change through Indonesian collective intrinsic design* (pp. 113-219). Springer VS. https://link.springer.com/book/10.1007/978-3-658-49695-1

# Reading Notes
## SQR3
### Survey: Skim the text and identify main ideas
- This chapter focuses on presenting the findings of fieldwork conducted in this book, which was conducted with grassroots design and innovation practitioners in Indonesia

- Sipos opens by surveying core literature in critical making scholarship, most heavily from Garnet Hertz, and then argues that this scholarship needs to be extended to community based contexts where grassroots practitioners are engaging in design and making work in spaces typically not captured in critical making research

- She summarizes her experience with several maker workshops and conferences that ultimately affirmed Sipos' perspective that existing critical making frameworks were not sufficiently accounting for making work taking place outside of Western spaces

- She then presents the design of her field research in Java, Indonesia, where she:
    - Mapped out several makerspace and grassroots innovation communities in the area and engaged in field visits to those sites
    - Conducted a participatory action research project (PAR) with one of the maker communities at these sites
    - Due to the pandemic, she ultimately planned a hackathon with one of these communities

- Based on the observations of her field research, she argues that her data is representative of the pluriversal nature of making and innovation practices across this community, drawing on cultural knowledge variably in different spaces, including those that were more urban or rural. She additionally highlights the unique design challenges faced by practitioners in Indonesia, responding to problems resulting from the colonial history of their land. This is exemplified in her analysis of numerous projects that were mapped during her fieldwork.

- She then offers two case studies to focus her discussion and analysis on:
    - "This first case study investigates a West Javanese grassroots innovation collective that acts as a supporting network for Indonesian grassroots communities. It also engages in maintaining community-based internet infrastructure (better known as _community network_) projects and organizing rural ICT camps for skill sharing" (p. 154)
    - The second case study involves description of the _Art-Science Collective_, "a Central Javanese collective that has been at the forefront of critical making for decades. This first part aims to shed light on how the same collective can experiment with terraforming a volcano, contribute to space research, and organize a civic hackathon in a pandemic... This collective initiated the second  layer in this analysis, the intrinsic design case study of the #hackfromhome hackathon" (p. 182)

### Query: Write questions to consider as you read

Given this, how is the notion of a decolonial perspective to design and participatory action research employed or potentially troubled in Sipos' analysis?

### Recite: Consider what you want to remember, and put that into your own words
The first portion of the chapter largely helps to lay the groundwork for explaining the timeline, theoretical focus and interests, and methods used for the author's field work in Indonesia. Here, Sipos explains how she found herself drawn to critical making largely because she felt like it reflected some of the critical making and crafting practices of grassroots communities comment especially in the Global South. She explains, however, that from her experience with attendees at hacker conferences and through mapping various maker spaces or grass roots practitioner sites throughout Indonesia , that reception and self identification with the term "critical making" varied. in some cases, practitioners prefer to think of themselves as engaging in technical practice , or hackers, or part of maker collectives, though there did still seem to be resonance with the critical making framework and ethos, especially as it has been described by Matt Ratto and Garnet Hertz.

following from her survey of numerous grass roots collectives and maker spaces, The author focuses in on two case studies. In the case of the first case study, She focused on a community based Internet infrastructure collective, which was established following the fall of the New Order regime that had been in place between 1967 and 19 98 under the leadership of President Suharto, whose regime was known to be Sinophobic, ethno nationalist, anti communist and anti socialist, and Islamophobic, And participated in the mass massacre of hundreds of thousands of Indonesians between 1965 and 1966, the effects of which would ripple throughout Indonesia and many of the research study sites covered in the author's work to the present day . in this case, once the regime fell, the collective sought to respond to the increasing access to Internet and communication technologies post 2001 . The author traces the origins and development of the Network Collective, considering the attentiveness to Eco Spiritual ontology of many Indonesian communities, interest in sustainable and appropriate uses of Internet and communication technologies that are affordable and ecologically conscious, and centering the needs of Indonesian indigenous communities over the desires of NGOs, non-profits, corporations, or other outside forces. 

The second case study explored the planning and unfolding of a remote hackathon that took place during the Covid 19 pandemic in response to community concerns about lack of access to PPE , the economic strains forest upon many people inequitably due to quarantine and social distancing, an existing social disparities that were heightened as a result of this global health crisis. Here, she illustrates how organizers had to grapple with their own local investment in sustainability with the urgency of this health crisis, which led to considerations about whether to make use of hackathon platforms that required users to buy into uses of their data that they otherwise would not have, questions about how to support the long term development of the prototypes that emerged from the hackathon, and how to adjust to the physical and technological barriers of using remote and digital technologies for this event in the midst of the pandemic.

## Intervention, Argument, Evidence
### Intervention: What is the intervention that the scholar is making, and where is that intervention being made? In other words, how do you place that intervention relative to existing disciplinary conversations that are taking place?
The author largely wanted to respond to a gap that she sees in critical making scholarship that does not seem to account for making that engages with criticality and contacts outside of academic institutions and formal research scholarship, of which there already is an undue burden or barrier faced by academics in the global South, but which also overlooks local and grassroots innovation, design, and making practices. Here, she largely draws on Matt Ratto's and Garnett Hertz's description of critical making.

### Argument: What are the core argument(s) being made in the text?
The most primary point that emerges from the chapter overall, and is supported in the analysis and findings of the two case studies, suggests that grassroots practitioners in local communities, both within and outside of the global South, May or may not find the term critical making to be adequate for explaining their orientation to materials, criticality, And technology, especially given the deeply ontological nature of design (Escobar, 2018) and thus the link between making and any communities' onto epistemologies.
