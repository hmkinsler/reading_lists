### Recite: Consider what you want to remember, and put that into your own words
In this article, Ratto outlines critical making as an approach for bridging gaps between conceptual ideas an engagement with the materiality of technology. He offers a three step approach for engaging in this critical making work: (1) Surveying the relevant literature, theories, and approaches that might shape the design of a given prototype, (2) the process of developing that prototype, often collaboratively, and (3) critical discussion, analysis, and / or reflection on that process.

### Relate: Identify connections or links to other readings and scholarship
At one point in the article, as Ratto is explaining his Flwr Pwr workshop, given that the flower microcontrollers were intended to illustrate concepts relevant to network theory, I saw a lot of connection to an alignment with some of Jane Bennett's (2010) work, especially in chapter two. He talks about the heterogeneous nature of the Flwr network, with some Flwrs transmitting more than they receive, and vice versa. Here, if we think of this as a kind of Flwr network assemblage , we can see that no one Flwr as an actant operates alone. Instead, the agency of the network is distributed unevenly.

I did find myself somewhat uncertain about his argument in terms of his criticism that prior engagements with technology have been overly conceptual or abstract. At one point, he even says " my goal is to make concepts more apprehendable, to bring them in ways to the body, not only the brain, and to leverage student and researchers personal experiences to make new connections between the lived space of the body and the conceptual space of scholarly knowledge" (p. 254). To Be sure, I can certainly agree that there are pedagogical limitations to the way that we teach about highly technical concepts, especially in the humanities and social sciences, where there is less institutional support to help students and researchers acquire that technological or digital literacy. At the same time, claims such as this one do run the risk of reifying the kinds of mind-body dualist perspectives that position one phenomenological-epistemological mode over the other.

Furthermore, though I think his emphasis on process over product is certainly worth exploring particularly in a social milieu where technological functionality is prioritize over exploration, messiness, and non-hegemonic engagements with technology, care needs to be taken to ensure that the implications for what is designed with a given prototype is never left to the wayside. As we know that the effects of technology are not felt equally across populations, a critical active engagement with technology also necessitates an interrogation of the politics of design, asking what is at stake when a design is being developed, and what the (un)intended consequences are for any technological development work.

## Intervention, Argument, Evidence
### Intervention: What is the intervention that the scholar is making, and where is that intervention being made? In other words, how do you place that intervention relative to existing disciplinary conversations that are taking place?
Ratto seems to be suggesting that there is a gap between understanding Technologies and their relationship to social life conceptually as opposed to engaging with those technologies materially. He views critical making then, for its affective and embodied affordances, as a practice that makes these connections more apparent.

### Argument: What are the core argument(s) being made in the text?
Essentially, Ratto suggests that critical making offers an important dimension to Pedagogical and research practice in terms of engagement with technology, particularly in attempting to understand the relationship of technology and materials to social life. This "theory-technology mapping" (p. 257), as he puts it, Helps to address limitations posed by merely abstract or conceptual engagements with technology and the material world .

### Evidence: What evidence is used to support the argument(s)?
Rato looks at two different workshops that he led, commenting on the challenges and outcomes for each of them , especially in terms of the degree to which workshop participants successfully made connections between the hands on making activities that they engaged in with the conceptual ideas that they attempted to interrogate. In the case of the first workshop, he had participants experiment with motors and pencils and the like to make a "drawbot", relying on the use of digital resources and guides to direct their maker process, in the hopes that This would open up commentary about issues related to distance education learning. In this case, however, Ratto Found that participants seemingly struggled with making the connection between their own engagement with digital resources and materials in order to learn how to engage in this maker process and make use of the materials that were provided to them with their stances on the efficacy of distance education learning. In the case of the second workshop, however, Ratto supplied "flwr" Microcontrollers that could transmit and receive infrared signals, using the continuation or failure of the flowers to transmit these signals to help illustrate concepts relevant to network theory, especially as workshop participants reflected on the way in which the flower successfully or unsuccessfully mediated these theoretical concepts through their technological behavior.

### How: What methods and theories are used to make the intervention or argument and support it?
Descriptive analysis, narrative account.

Draws most heavily on scholarship in STS (Latour, Haraway), social construction of technology (Wajcman, Bijker), and constructionism (Papert, Knorr-Cetina)
