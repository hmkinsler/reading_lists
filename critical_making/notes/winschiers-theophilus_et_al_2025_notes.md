# Reading Notes
## SQR3
### Survey: Skim the text and identify main ideas
- They describe the aims of the chapter as offering "contributions from Participatory Design and decolonizing movements and how they engage across four strands: the anticolonial, postcolonial, decolonial, and the pluriversal. Each of the strands addresses attempts to reconcile different worlds, knowledges and epistemologies that can contribute towards new framings and directions for (de)colonialities in Participatory Design" (p. 111)

- The first section opens with a survey of various "strands" of decolonial approaches to design, considering the development of Participatory Design methods as one potential response that accounts for locally-informed design that still faces limitations due to issues related to lack of deep engagement with Indigenous epistemologies, Eurocentric methods, misrepresentation of Indigeneity, etc. For this reason, they argue that the historicity of decolonial Participatory Design research is of critical importance for resolving such issues, hence the four-strand approach they take in the chapter.

*Anticolonial Strand*
Here, they focus on anticolonialism as an approach that seeks to reject the hegemony of Western epistemologies in favor of local knowledge practices. In this case, Scandinavian participatory design research as well as Freire's critical pedagogy emphasize local epistemologies as key in actively shaping the design process. They explain, "the anticolonial strand is characterized by strong past and present engagements with social movements fighting for emancipation, national liberation, information technology sovereignty, workplace rights and peer-to-peer commoning" (p. 116). In other words, anticolonial efforts are those that are explicitly focused on movements in anathema (_anti_) colonialism, calling to mind anticolonial movements like those in Tanzania in the late-60s to mid-70s, of which Walter Rodney took part. Here, anticolonial scholarship is explicitly concerned with this emancipatory political movement that criticizes and seeks to dismantle colonialism in its many forms.

*Postcolonial Strand*
This strand "emphasises the unsettling or rupture with new forms of colonialism, such as long-standing theoretical discourses of postcoloniality and challenges of 'modernity' and development aid coming from former metropolises, in favor of local initiatives based on situated knowledge(s)" (p. 113). This strand thus focuses specifically on contexts where communities are responding to colonial legacies following liberation and independence, and a resulting reorientation to global power asymmetries. This aligns with scholarship concerned with discourses of development where colonial forces attempt to reify their formed power dynamics through NGO initiatives, the re-asserting of Western dominance on the structuring of the global economy through the World Bank, and savior-saved discourses that position postcolonial communities as in constant need of Western education, aid, and developmental support in order to "achieve" modernity as it has been prescribed for them.

*Decolonial Strand*
Winschiers-Theophilus et al. (2025) write that "decolonial approaches assume that colonialities are historical and structural products of colonial societies that will never go away, hence the need to keep a constant production of decolonialities. Instead of favoring a rupture with the colonial ways as earlier anticolonial intellectuals fought for, decolonial intellectuals are more interested in problematizing the uneasy conviviality of indigenous, colonisers, forced immigrants and settlers in former colonies and metropolises" (p. 119). In these contexts, decolonial efforts focuse on tensions that arise from context between colonizers and Indigenous groups, or those who do not fit neatly into either category, taking all people to task for their role in actively deconstructing colonial logics and their consequences, the politics of their onto-epistemologies.

*Pluriversal Strand*
This strand notably has some overlap with decolonial strands of participatory design, but maintains a much more explicit focus on imagining otherwise beyond the colonial onto-epistemologies that are imposed on communities. This requires an interrogation of political ontologies (Escobar, 2018) and moves toward alternative world-building, in which "Participatory Design researchers are trying to address issues such as environmental crisis, indigenous peoples' existential conditions and rights of the land, global power struggles, oppressions and wars" (p. 114).

### Query: Write questions to consider as you read
- How does this four-strand approach to issues of decoloniality in participatory design perhaps offer eludication or even answers to the problem of the term "decolonial" being collapsed in with "anticolonial" scholarship as described in Shivers-McNair (2021) and Itchuaqiyaq and Matheson (2021)?

- Does the _pluriversal_ strand described by Winschiers-Theophilus et al. (2025) actually more closely align with Tuck and Yang's (2012) and Inuqiyaq and Matheson's (2023) definition of _decolonial_, given the latter scholars' argument that decolonial scholarship must be materially bound to strive toward rematriation and Indigenous sovereignty, especially over land?

- Relatedly, is there any sound reasons behind this collapsing? Are there more holistic frameworks that could help bring these related strands together without fully reducing the nuanced differences between decolonial, anticolonial, and postcolonial perspectives?
