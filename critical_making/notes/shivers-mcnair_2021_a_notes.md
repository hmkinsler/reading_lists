## Intervention, Argument, Evidence
### Intervention: What is the intervention that the scholar is making, and where is that intervention being made? In other words, how do you place that intervention relative to existing disciplinary conversations that are taking place?
As the opening to her book, this introduction surveys and brings together the various bodies of scholarship that Shivers-McNair seeks to respond to and build on in her longitudinal, ethnographic fieldwork with a makerspace in Seattle. She traces the various disciplinary areas that interest in the multimodal affordances of making, including rhetoric and writing studies, composition , and professional and technical communication . Her work, in particular, seeks to explore the relational nature of maker practices:

"This book takes up these conversations and focuses on relations and boundary-marking practices in acts of making---especially what counts as making and who counts as a maker. These boundaries are not fixed or absolute, but are marked (and re-marked) through relations and interactions among people, things, technologies, communities, meanings, spaces, and disciplines. In other words, boundary marks and relations both shape and are shaped by what gets made (and what doesn't), by whom, and for whom. Analyzing and understanding these marks, then, can help researchers, teachers, and practitioners make informed decisions about why and how to engage with the maker movement in our communities" (pp. 16-17).

### Argument: What are the core argument(s) being made in the text?
Part of the overall goal for her relational rhetorics framework, she argues, results from the tendency of critical maker practitioners to privileged technological and digital forms of making over local maker practices, marginalizing the maker and design practices of women, the LGBTQIA+ community, disabled people, Black and brown communities, and Indigenous peoples in the process.

"...this book models a relational framework for understanding and participating in acts of making. This framework operates at multiple levels: the making and remaking of knowledge across theories, perspectives, and experiences: the making and remaking of bodies, relationships, meanings, technologies and the makerspace itself; and the making and remaking of practices across local maker communities, the global maker movement, and maker-inspired pedagogies. At each level, I highlight relations and boundary marks that are dynamic, collaborative, multimodal, material, and culturally situated. In other words, while this book is a study of acts of making in a makerspace, it is also a study of how the boundaries of 'maker,' 'making,' and 'knowledge' both shape and are shaped by relations among bodies, technologies, traditions, materials, things, and spaces" (p. 22).

### Evidence: What evidence is used to support the argument(s)?
She provides an overview of what each chapter in the book covers and how it contributes to our overall argument:
    - Chapter open focuses on introducing the concept of relational rhetorics
    - Chapter two considers the use of stories as a method(ology) for capturing and analyzing the unfolding of relational rhetorics in maker spaces
    - Chapter three explores the story of Shivers-McNair's and others' attempts to ingratiate themselves into the makerspace and recognize themselves as makers
    - Chapter four considers how moments of "disequilibrium" or breakdown in makerspaces are taken up as a generative source for invention by makers
    - Chapter five "focuses on professional practice in the maker movement and intersecting technology industries" (p. 25)
    - Chapter six explores Shivers-McNair's development of a digital rhetorics course that emerged from and was informed by her own research on making and makerspaces, considering the pedagogical implications of making for students of rhetoric
    - The conclusion reflects on the major findings of her study, its limitations, and areas for future research
