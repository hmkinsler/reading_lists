### Survey: Skim the text and identify main ideas

- The introduction for the book sets forth the stakes for Shipka's argument , especially her concern that recent work in writing, rhetoric, and composition studies has been moving toward a collapsing of terms like multimodality, intertextuality, multimedia, and media rich as stand-ins for texts that are in some way, shape, or form technological or digital. Here, Is where Shipka outlines her interest in developing a more substantial or full account of multimodal composition, both in terms of the process in composing takes place, and in terms of the texts that emerge from that process.

- Chapter one explores two arguments:
    - That composition studies must necessarily shift toward an account of composition beyond written discourse alone.
    - That this shift toward theorizing and researching composition must be careful "against facilitating changes that result in the substitution of one set of sign systems, technologies, and limitations for another or that privilege certain ways of knowing, learning, and composing while denigrating or downplaying the value of othes" (p. 14)

- Chapter two "Examines how a sociocultural approach to communicative practice provides us with ways of attending to the social and individual aspects of composing processes without losing sight of the wide variety of genres, sign systems, technologies that composers routinely employ while creating texts" (p. 14). Here, she introduces a mediated action framework that draws on James Wertsch's scholarship to focus analysis on "the _individual(s)-interacting-with-mediational-means_" (p. 14).

-  Chapter three demonstrates the applicability of the mediated action framework to two composing process studies, illustrating how composition is situated within a complex mesh of related activities and actors that influence the composition process.

- Chapter four extends this framework further, providing a pedagogical approach that refuses predetermination of the means by which students compose while still developing their rhetorical awareness: 

    "The framework highlights the interconnectedness of systems of production, distribution, reception, and circulation by providing students with complex tasks and activities that require them to consider how the contexts in which texts participate shape the way those texts are received and responded to" (p.15).

She analyzes two vignettes of working with first-year composition students to illustrate the efficacy of this framework and pedagogical approach.

- Chapter five continues in this line of framework development, with a specific focus on how to assess and respond to multimodal composition without maintaining a sole focus on digital texts or multimodal pieces that make use of new or emerging technologies. Here, a great emphasis is placed on student's role in the evaluative process, carrying through a current of investment on student autonomy and active participation in shaping the composition process from start to end throughout this book.

#### Introduction
In the introduction, Shipka basically frames what feels like a turning point or a conflict in rhetoric and composition studies in which scholars are unsure of how to tackle the problem of multimodality and technology in their classrooms, especially at a time where they know that there will never be enough that can be covered within the course of a semester or a year to fully prepare students for all of the writing situations or academic genres that they will encounter following their first year composition course . This raises questions about the efficacy of first year writing classes, in terms of whether they are touching on enough of the genre and composition skills that are required for students to be successful in the latter years of their undergraduate degrees but also in preparing for the workforce or any future endeavors they have that me be beyond the scope of higher education institutions. Here, Shipka surveys The field contemporary perspective on multimodality and technology, pointing out that there are still limitations in the way that written and academic texts are privileged over other forms of writing, especially in ways that fail to account for the embodied and situated nature in which composition work actually takes place . She writes, "theoretical methodological, and pedagogical frameworks that fail to trace the complex ways that texts come to be, and overlook how writing functions as but one 'stream within the broader flows of semiotic activity'... cannot help but fail to illuminate the roles other texts, talk, people, perceptions, semiotic resources , technologies, motives, activities, and institutions play in the production, reception, circulation, and valuation of seemingly stable finished texts" (p. 13).

#### Chapter One
in this first chapter, Shipka dives further into her interrogation of rhetoric and composition scholars perspectives on the meaning of technology and multimodality , especially to trouble the idea that technology specifically denotes the digital or electronic technologies that we frequently associate with academic work in writing, such as use of word processing softwares, software, tablets and cell phones, and so on. She points out that there are many other things that come into play as technologies that shape the composition process, such as student desks and chairs, pencils, paper, lights, etc. In other words, she reminds us that while we can "remain both cognizant of and optimistic about the ways newer technologies promise to impact our research, scholarship, and pedagogical practice, a composition made whole requires us to be more mindful about our term of a use like _technology_" (pp. 20-21). Similarly, She interrogates the contemporary understanding of process, especially in composition research. Here, she argues that process writing is largely focused research on what we recognize as formal Written production of academic texts. She argues that this view fails to account for the more complex milieu in which composition actually takes place, obscuring researchers and scholars sense of how that work unfolds , and similarly misleading students as to the mediated, relational, and embodied aspects of composition work. She draws on the example of a scholar who is preparing for a conference paper as an example, who, as she is writing, will consult a myriad of other kinds of texts such as other publications and abstracts and the call for proposals, will talk with her colleagues and family and friends and peers, Will read through her text or seek out feedback from others or even read it aloud, who will annotate and write notes in response to other texts that she feels are relevant and worth addressing in her own writing, who will make changes to formatting, who will write in a myriad of places whether they may be her cubicle desk or her apartment or at a coffee shop or at the library or outside at a park. Ultimately, Shipka's argument here is that a more holistic sense of composition will allow us to not only look to a greater range of objects and actors and materials and mediators that are relevant to the composition process, but it will also encompass more of the actions and contexts and environments in which composition takes place.

#### Chapter Two
The second chapter largely focuses on introducing the notion of _mediated action,_ drawing on James Wertsch's (1998) _Mind as action,_ In which words seeks to understand the relationship between a given individual and the sociocultural settings or tools with which they engage. She cites Wertsch's Call to understand individuals cognition and psychological processes as interrelated to these socio cultural environments: "Analytic strategies often take one of two general paths, depending on what is given analytic primacy. One path is grounded in the assumption that it is appropriate to begin with an account of societal phenomena and, on the basis of these phenomena, generate analysis of individual mental functioning; the other assumes that the way to understand societal phenomena is to start with the psychological or other processes carried out by the individual" (Wertsch, 1998, as cited in Shipka, 2011, pp. 41-42). Such a framework allows for analysts to both consider the individual who is engaging in action that is mediated in some way, as well as the mediational means by which that action or work occurs. From here, Shipka takes up several characteristics that Wertsch describes as associated with mediated action that are relevant for her own pedagogical framework:

    "The four characteristics of mediated action that I will treat below are: (1) mediated action typically serves multiple purposes or goals; (2) mediated action is simultaneously enabled and constrained by mediational means; (3) mediated action is historically situated; and (4) mediated action is transformed with the introduction of new mediational means" (pp. 43-44).

#### Chapter Three
In this chapter, Shipka attempts to apply her mediated action framework to a couple of examples from her own composition class , where students were tasked with creating visual representations of their composition processes. In one case, a student creates a sketch in which she is reacting to the directions for the assignment that are being provided to her by Shepka, depicting herself as uncertain or confused about how she should respond to the writing prompt until she is pictured calling a friend and then having a kind of "lightbulb moment" that sets her on the next step of her writing process. Through these examples, shipka is able to capture the more complex and varied factors that ultimately shaped her students experiences working on their assignments in that class.

#### Chapter Four
She then shifts into explaining some of the tensions or apprehensions that many scholars in composition have about embracing writing assignments that they see as radically multimodal and divergent from the typical, formal, written genres that first year composition students are ask to tackle . Shipka acknowledges that there is a lot of uncertainty about the efficacy of any given pedagogical approach in terms of what kinds of genres that students are asked to respond to, but she sees this uncertainty as a potential opening for exploring the affordances of centering meta-communicative and rhetorical awareness as opposed to privileging anyone genre over another. She essentially makes the argument that when students are posed a problem that could be responded to in a wide variety of ways, in a wide variety of genres and modalities, and through a variety of compositional approaches and techniques, then they are taking on more of the rhetorical work that we often see as we continue to progress through our professional careers, whether in the academy or an industry. In a sense, her argument here is not wholly aligned with but potentially is related to the notion of _tranfer._ By this I mean to argue that shipka suggesting that we could task our students with any given number of academic genres, whether annotated bibliographies, argumentative essays, Imrad style research papers , and the like, but the question will remain about whether or not there encountering of these genres ultimately enables them to adapt to new formal written genres in the future. alternatively, in allowing students to make the final determination about what kind of a genre is most appropriate for responding to a given problem, They may develop the rhetorical and communicative awareness that is transferable to a wide variety writing situations in the future, whether those that involve formal writing or otherwise.

#### Chapter Five
given the wide variety of student work that is possible with this pedagogical framework, Shipka highlights the importance of students role in their own assessment, providing what she calls a _statement of goals and choices_ (SOGC) In which students are tasked with explaining Their goals in responding to an assignment prompt or problem , as well as explicating and defending the rhetorical choices that they made in the development of their text, regardless of the modality or shape of that text.
