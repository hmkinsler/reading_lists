# Reading Notes
## SQR3
### Survey: Skim the text and identify main ideas
- The introduction opens with the author narrating their experience traveling through an airport and confronting the adequacies of the design for TSA checkpoint technologies to account for trans embodiment as a concrete example of the intersection between trans experiences and discriminatory design practices

- They trace the inception and development of the Design Justice Network, a community made up of scholars and researchers who seek to promote more equitable practices in design

- They offer some framing for the overall book in the introduction, including their positionality statement and their focus on participatory action research as methodology of choice

- from there, They provide extended definitions of various key concepts and terms that are the focus of the books theoretical and analytical frameworks , including "design", "intersectionality", "the matrix of domination", and "design justice"

- How does the author think about design justice in the context of participatory action research, especially as opposed to participatory design research?

### Relate: Identify connections or links to other readings and scholarship
There are really compelling connections to Black feminist and Indigenous scholarship in this text, which I found interesting because it can sometimes be challenging to express in writing the relationship between feminist theory and praxis, especially given the way that technology is increasingly conceptualized in immaterial ways. With that, I see this piece as, like Gollihue (2019), bridging the work of decolonial participatory researchers (Hayhurst, 2025; Becerra, 2026) as they are informed by Escobar's (2018) notion of pluriversality into conversation with critical design pedagogy. Here, I did find myself intrigued about how to read this work in conversation with Tham (2020), and I especially wonder where there might need to be more specific focus on design justice frameworks and principles and practices in a context where we may attempt to bring a greater focus on hand-on training and design skills for students in technical communication.

## Intervention, Argument, Evidence
### Intervention: What is the intervention that the scholar is making, and where is that intervention being made? In other words, how do you place that intervention relative to existing disciplinary conversations that are taking place?

### Argument: What are the core argument(s) being made in the text?
As an introduction to the book, much of this chapter is focused on introducing key terms and scholarship from which the larger argument of the text is based, specifically with a focus on considering how a design justice framework informs design theory and practice in a way that is resistant to dominant norms present in the matrix of domination (including white supremacist, settler-colonial logics) and instead allows for community-rooted speculative design practices that resist, subvert, refuse, or imagine otherwise in response to these systems of oppression.

### Evidence: What evidence is used to support the argument(s)?
- Each chapter focuses on different issues that are relevant for design justice theorists and practioners, whether in terms of the values that are encoded in design, the narratives or practices shaping who is engaging in design, the sites where design work takes place, and how design justice is taught. In each case, the author draws on examples from design justice practitioners in the field, whether encountering algorithmic bias and its consequences in facial recognition technologies, or in student and teacher experiences in critical design classes.

### How: What methods and theories are used to make the intervention or argument and support it?
The author brings up several projects that are either born out of or related to the Design Justice Network, and related scholarship focused on issues of design bias (i.e. Boulamwini, Noble). In doing so, she highlights Black feminist theorists and scholars (i.e. Crenshaw, Collins) to explain how design justice involves first being able to identify the systemic forces of oppression that shape design beyond the single-axis approaches that have historically hindered "universal" design practice.
