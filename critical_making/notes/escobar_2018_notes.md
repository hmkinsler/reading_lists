### Survey: Skim the text and identify main ideas
- Book is organized in three key parts: (1) a survey of design literature and scholarship on cultural studies approaches to design, (2) an analysis of the ontological politics of design, (3) and a proposal for the theoretical concept of _pluriversality._

- Plusiversality entails a design framework and practice that shifts away from discourses of development rooted in white, Western, settler-colonial logics for design that are imposed on the Global South toward a recognition that multiple, simultaneous ontological approaches to design exist, and that subaltern design practices must be understood in their own right, particularly to imagine a more just future that rejects neoliberal and colonial technoideologies.

- Chapter one focuses on literature that has explored several alternative models for design, informed by cultural studies. Here, he looks at the relationship between identity, technology, and politics as they inform design in various historical contexts, such as the display of emerging and new technologies at world fairs. He explains participatory and community-centered design practices and their political stakes in constrast to top-down principles of design, exploring how this shift toward critical design practices allows for a more entangled, embodied, and situated account of the implications of design.

- Chapter two traces debates about design theory and practice in various disciplinary areas, including "anthropology, ecology, architecture and urbanism, digital studies, development studies, political ecology, and feminist theory" (p. 3).

- Chapter three considers the implications for taking on a minor theory approach to design, especially in the context of dominant ideologies that subjugate relational ontological perspectives in favor of rationalist and dualist perspectives. Here, he interrogates the rational and cartestian tradition's investment in individualism, objectivity, empiricist, positivist, and neoliberal perspectives that have been weaponized against marginalized populations, especially in the Global South.

- Chapter four outlines the stakes for ontological perspectives on design. Here, he concludes with a list of key characteristics and committments that underlie an ontological approach to design.

- Chapter five considers the meaning of pluriversality in the history and development of design practice globally.

- Chapter six considers the implications for autonomous design specifically, with Escobar arguing that "the basic insight is, again, seemingly straightforward: that every community practices the design of itself" (p. 5).

- The conclusion offers a call to action to shift to a more ontologically informed, transitional, relational, and ecologically concerned design practice.

#### Part I | Design for the real world: But which "world?" What "design?" What "real?"

##### Chapter One
Much of the work done in this chapter focuses on historicizing research and scholarship perspectives on design leading up to the presentation of Escobar's argument for autonomous design or more plural perspectives on design. Here, he argues that regardless of your person starts in describing this trajectory for mapping the history of contemporary design, the ultimately are tasked with confronting the consequences of modernist perspectives on design, technology, and materials. He looks at various strains of scholarship that attempt to respond to the issue of modernism and Design, including work exploring the possibilities of vernacular architecture, alternative designs that reject modernist models of the city, Critical interrogation of the geopolitics underlying the materiality and production of digital Technologies and media, explorations into ecological and sustainable models for design, Speculative design, critical design studies, and participatory models for design.

##### Chapter Two
This chapter Builds on the previous by looking to scholarship in adjacent or connected disciplinary areas for their affordances and limitations and contributing to critical perspectives on design, especially for the purpose of establishing a cultural studies of design. He states, “these fields include anthropology, development studies, political ecology, and feminist theory” (p. 51)

In his own summary of the chapter:

    The first, and main, part of the chapter focuses on the diverse engagements between anthropology and design, alternating between the more applied or action-oriented “design anthropology” and the critical “anthropology of design.” The second part moves on to consider recent design inroads into the fields of development and humanitarian aid; it looks at the ways in which designers are attempting to position themselves within development projects, including the rapidly growing, and distinctive, space of humanitarianism. Finally, the third part discusses the scholarly transformation, known as the ontological turn, that ensued from the encounter between the field of political ecology and the evolving concerns with (ontology objects, things, matter, the real, imminence, process) in postconstructivist social theory. Arising out of this intersection, the nascent field of political ontology, this chapter suggests, constitutes a constructive space for rethinking design ontologically (pp. 51-52)

He surveys scholarship in design anthropology, ethnography and design, and anthropology of design, considering how the intersection of anthropological theory and methods and design are generative in their articulation of the cultural politics of design. In other words, he argues that anthropological perspectives offer the insight that design is an act of world-building that is both culturally informed and informing culture in response. Furthermore, he highlights that anthropology’s attentiveness to multiple dimensions of human experience and culture, including in theorizing power, class, gender, race, sexuality, coloniality, and the like, are notable interventions for understanding the relationship between these dimensions of life and design.

He also highlights work seeking to respond to the problem of development discourse and development design thinking that saw a major rise in the latter half of the 20th century, with designers from the global north imposing Eurocentric models for modernity, rationality, etc. onto populations in the global south, reifying colonial dynamics in the process. For this reason, he argues, “A decolonial perspective on development is thus essential for approaching codesign with subaltern groups in ways that strengthen, rather than undermine, their collective autonomy. Only by attending to the entrenched geopolitics of development knowledge can designers become more critical of how design operates within unequal World orders and in the Borderlands of the modern/colonial world system and become a force for change alongside those groups most negatively affected by modern designs, grand or small” (p. 62).

Finally, he introduces veins of scholarship in feminist political ecology (FPE) and political ontology. In both cases, the scholarship is informed by a recognition of the hegemonic onto-epistemology that is Eurocentric and modernist in nature that is treated as the singular ontological or epistemological perspective to be imposed, which he likens to John Law’s “One-World-World” (oww). It is here that he identifies the ontological and political necessity for pluriversal perspectives, those which recognize the existence and affirm the futurality of many world.

#### Part II | The ontological reorientation of design

##### Chapter Three
It is in this chapter, then, that Escobar outlines the logic that underlies the oww perspective. He states, “the tradition we are talking about is variously referred to as rationalistic, cartesian, or objectivist is often associated with related terms such as mechanistic (worldview), reductionist (science), positivistic (epistemology), and more recently, computationalist (model)” (p. 80).

He thus offers an explanation of several core beliefs that are foundational to this modern onto-epistemological tradition:

- “The Belief in the Individual” (p. 83)
- "The Belief in the Real” (p. 85)
- “The Belief in Science” (p. 88)
- “The Belief in the Economy” (p. 89)

It is in resistance to this singular onto-epistemological perspective that Escobar sees the pluriversal possibilities for relational onto-epistemological models.

##### Chapter Four
Here, Escobar offers his ontological design framework, the necessity of which he states that Winograd and Flores do well to summarize:

    he most important design is ontological. It constitutes an intervention in the background of our heritage, growing out of our already-existent ways of being in the world, and deeply affecting the kinds of beings that we are. In creating new artifacts, equipment, buildings, and organizational structures, it attempts to specify in advance how and where breakdowns will show up in our everyday practices and in the tools we use, opening up new spaces in which we can work and play. Ontologically oriented design is therefore necessarily both reflective and political, looking back to the traditions that have formed us but also forwards to as-yet-uncreated transformations of our lives together. Through the emergence of new tools, we come to a changing awareness of human nature and human action, which in terms leads to new technological development. The designing process is part of this “dance” in which our structure of possibilities is generated. (1986, p. 163)

Additional aspects of the ontological issues at stake for design that he explores include the question of human design, posthuman perspectives, the problem of artificiality in grappling with human design, and the need to push toward models for designing sustainment rather than sustainability. Part of his response to these challenges with posthumanism or new materialism as underlying models for ontological design involves recognizing distributed agency in the design process.

Ultimately, some key principles of ontological design that he covers:

- It requires affirmation of relationality and reconnection with things, environments, nonhumans, etc.
- Design is ontological in that designers are creating their own many words, which are connected to each other
- Reject defuturing in favor of futuring
- Is rooted in posthumanist practice
- Promotes conviviality
- Is invested in responding to emergent possibilities to further move toward more connected, ecologically responsible, and grounded futures

#### Part III | Designs for the pluriverse

##### Design for transitions
He covers insights from the field of transition design / design for transitions / design for social innovation: “Share by most TDs is the contention that we need to step outside existing institutional and epistemic boundaries if we truly want to strive for worlds and practices capable of bringing about the significant transformations seen as needed” (p. 139)

Relatedly, he looks toward movements in degrowth and postdevelopment as ethical frameworks that can support or supplement TD frameworks, the former of which entails a rejection of modernist models for perpetual extraction, consumption, and growth that are unsustainable, and the latter of which seeks to undo the harm of Eurocentric logics for development that were forced onto peoples in postcolonial contexts. 

##### Autonomous design and the politics of relationality and the communal
He outlines a model for autonomous design, informed by the concept of autonomía, a communal system that seeks to live, as he describes, “beyond the logic of the State and capital by relying on, and creating, nonliberal, non-State, and noncapitalist forms of being, doing, and knowing” (p. 181). 

This design framework is defined by:
- Understanding that communities are themselves actively engaging in their own design
- Likewise, these communities (of designers) are enacting their own knowledge and expertise as they design
- The first goal of design is for the community to inquire into its own history, needs, future interests, and so on
- From this inquiry, the community-designers iteratively and recursively articulate the problems and possibilities at stake as they design
- They may build “a model of the system that generates the problem of communal concern” (p. 185)

### Evidence: What evidence is used to support the argument(s)?
I felt like this book was a challenging read because there are _so_ many threads that Escobar is weaving together, which is both hard to summarize succinctly but is also a wonderful demonstration of the form mediating the very content of his argument. With that, I would say that the evidence provided in this book, of which there is a great deal of literature review and synthesis, progresses through each chapter.

In other words, he starts out with literature mapping the rise of contemporary scholarship on design. Then he explains the need to draw on relational ontologies (chapter two) to address the limitations of the dominant modern onto-epistemological order (chapter three). This, then, he takes as the stakes for proposing ontologically oriented models for design (chapter four), from which he offers various specific frameworks that are take up this relational onto-epistemological vision, including transition design (chapter five), and autonomous design (chapter six).
