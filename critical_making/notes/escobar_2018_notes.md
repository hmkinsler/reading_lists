# Reading Notes
## SQR3
### Survey: Skim the text and identify main ideas
- Book is organized in three key parts: (1) a survey of design literature and scholarship on cultural studies approaches to design, (2) an analysis of the ontological politics of design, (3) and a proposal for the theoretical concept of _pluriversality._

- Plusiversality entails a design framework and practice that shifts away from discourses of development rooted in white, Western, settler-colonial logics for design that are imposed on the Global South toward a recognition that multiple, simultaneous ontological approaches to design exist, and that subaltern design practices must be understood in their own right, particularly to imagine a more just future that rejects neoliberal and colonial technoideologies.

- Chapter one focuses on literature that has explored several alternative models for design, informed by cultural studies. Here, he looks at the relationship between identity, technology, and politics as they inform design in various historical contexts, such as the display of emerging and new technologies at world fairs. He explains participatory and community-centered design practices and their political stakes in constrast to top-down principles of design, exploring how this shift toward critical design practices allows for a more entangled, embodied, and situated account of the implications of design.

- Chapter two traces debates about design theory and practice in various disciplinary areas, including "anthropology, ecology, architecture and urbanism, digital studies, development studies, political ecology, and feminist theory" (p. 3).

- Chapter three considers the implications for taking on a minor theory approach to design, especially in the context of dominant ideologies that subjugate relational ontological perspectives in favor of rationalist and dualist perspectives. Here, he interrogates the rational and cartestian tradition's investment in individualism, objectivity, empiricist, positivist, and neoliberal perspectives that have been weaponized against marginalized populations, especially in the Global South.

- Chapter four outlines the stakes for ontological perspectives on design. Here, he concludes with a list of key characteristics and committments that underlie an ontological approach to design.

- Chapter five considers the meaning of pluriversality in the history and development of design practice globally.

- Chapter six considers the implications for autonomous design specifically, with Escobar arguing that "the basic insight is, again, seemingly straightforward: that every community practices the design of itself" (p. 5).

- The conclusion offers a call to action to shift to a more ontologically informed, transitional, relational, and ecologically concerned design practice.
