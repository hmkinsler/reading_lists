### Survey: Skim the text and identify main ideas
- This is a bit of an unusual text because it is comprised of multiple zines that make up a singular book, And each of the individual zines cover different topics or aspects of critical making scholarship

![Various individual zines from the _Critical making_ book project are depicted spread out on a table.](critical_making\images\critical-making--covers-800x536.jpg)

- The first of the zines, titled "Introduction" offers an introduction to the book project and also an introduction to critical making scholarship more generally 

- The second zine, "Manifestos," Includes various kinds of manifestes from critical making practitioners including Carl DiSalvo, Norman White, Anne Burdick, Garnet Hertz, etc. In some cases, the manifestos focus on different issues specific to critical making, including critical engineering, adversarial design, and working with networks .

- The third zine, "Terms," Reads as a kind of avant garde glossary of terms and major events in the maker movement, with writers covering historical events such as the Occupy movement, or explaining terms that are relevant for specific contacts and use cases, such as business design Or maker practices in the steampunk movement and other countercultures or subculture communities.

- The fourth zine, "Projects," offers a sampling of a variety of critical making projects, offering both analysis and takeaways from the maker process, and also guides or models for how to approach developing larger critical making projects.

- The fifth zine, "Conversations," includes a series of interviews between Garnet Hertz and various critical making scholars, including Matt Ratto, Natalie Jeremijenko, Alex Galloway, and Phoebe Sengers.

- The sixth zine, "Places," focuses on critical making projects centering about space and place in particular, including DIY culture in specific locations such as Jube and Jamaica, or projects that explore mobility and biking or lowriding in various cities.

- The seventh zine, "Science," considers the political relationship between science, technology, and society when viewed through a critical making lens. They consider the affordances of making for DIY citizen science initiatives and projects, as well as the ontological and political implications for crowdsourcing data and hacking or other forms of resistance to top-down scientific research apparatuses.

- The eight zine, "Make," looks at the spaces and environments in which maker practice takes place, whether in Maker Faires, Makerspaces, or other community making initiatives and events.

- The ninth zine, "Childhood," explores reflections on the embodied experiences of makers as they age, reflecting on their own orientation to making in childhood.

- The tenth zine, "History," considers historical documentation that depicts maker practice dating back more than one hundred years, considering how maker culture has changed in the last century.

- The final zine, "Envelope," is mostly a space for maker works to be disseminated, including various stickers and a crowdsourced map developed for the RideSouthLA project that explored issues of micromobility and biking.

### Recite: Consider what you want to remember, and put that into your own words
This book project, or compilation of several zines, Felt relevant less for all of the arguments or theories that are invoked throughout the text by the many contributors to this project , and much more relevant because of the Way that the text is representative of ongoing disciplinary conversations, debates, cultural and political shifts , and institutional issues facing scholars, researchers, artists, scientists, engineers, designers, business owners, and others who identify themselves in some way as part of the maker community, especially as "critical" makers.

The project was spearheaded by Garnet Hertz, Who explained that he was inspired to work on this project after witnessing The unfolding of public debate between Tim O'reilly and Mitch Altman following The news that the Maker Faire would be receiving a substantial grant from DARPA, the Defense Advanced Research Projects Agency, a research organization for the United States Department of Defense. Mitch Altman, A volunteer who had supported the Maker Faire for many years and had received awards and recognition for doing so, publicly announced on Facebook that he would not be attending or supporting Maker Fares so long as they continue to receive this funding . M O'Reilly, Who is the head of O'reilly Media , the parent company that overseas the Maker Faire, responded with a great deal of frustration to Altman publicly, an incident that many contributors to this book project recognized as emblematic of a trend in which the Make Magazine and Maker Faire seemed largely interested in maker projects that were depoliticized. Throughout the book, it becomes apparent that many of the earliest contributors to these magazines and maker fares or maker scholarship within the academy feel that this trend toward techno solutionism or technoliberalism and alignment with corporate and government funding and investment stands anathema to the very origins and root of maker culture.

This tension between a corporate and government allied maker culture and a resistant political ontology of making is apparent throughout the book . Some contributors talk about their interest in Scrappiness, the kind of maker knowledge that they saw in their parents and grandparents as they often had to rely and build things out of necessity , their hope to reject the imposition of technological obsolescence, and so on. Part of what I enjoyed about this book so much is that it truly captured the elements of maker culture that I resonate with the most deeply. And when I was 16 years old, I started Zine website, which sought to function as a glossary and routing source for other locations where zines could be found. I started the website partially because of my fear of these zines somehow being lost overtime, and I thought of it as a kind of personal way back machine or Internet archive for these texts that I felt like connected me to a community of organizers and political activists from years and decades prior. The Occupy movement took place when I was 11 years old, so when I began to Read this massive collection of zines about that movement as a high school student five years later , I felt like I was transported to a different time. They offered guidance on how to respond to riot cops and pepper spray and tear gas and rubber bullets, on what it means to be part of an affinity group, principles of cyber security and data privacy. the world that was described in these DIY publications felt very near and yet very far to the one that I lived in , in the year that Donald Trump was elected for his first term as president. I came across countless websites about the origins of hacker culture, I would read manifestos about what it means to hack or how one can hack for political good. Though I wasn't a programmer then, and I wouldn't call myself one now, so I couldn't understand the technical expertise that was being shared forums and self hosted web pages that I imagine look the same now as they did in the early 2000s when they were first made available, but I could very clearly sense the ethical and political imperatives that were being communicated in these documents. When I started attending punk shows shortly after, and created my first crust pants and began printing zines to distribute en masse, It was very clear to me what it means for maker culture to be rooted in DIY . On the one hand, yes, I found community with these people and felt drawn to these spaces digitally and physically for that reason, but on the other hand I would state that I felt myself pulled there out of necessity. For someone without much access to money, any political or expressive act required reliance on found materials, knowledge passed on through open-source spaces and archives. I learned very quickly the importance of mutual aid and community networks for the production and circulation of knowledge.

Shortly after I graduated community college, I learned that crime think, one of the largest Zine distribution publishers, had been IP blocked for all of the computers on that campus because of how often I had used the library printers to print and distribute hundreds of their zines across campus. 

All this to say that there is something incredibly temporal and historical about the work that was being done in this book . To me, Reading this as a Zine transports me back to that Community College library. It reminds me of all of the strange formatting experiments that I would have to engage in in order to get the booklets to print double sided correctly and to ensure that they were properly readable. It reminds me of all the annotations and notes that I would take about the DIY projects that many zines offer tutorials for, notably with lots of options for found or low-cost materials to be used. This text is notable because of the way that it captures tensions in the maker community at that time as they responded to the political moments of their own time, and it offers ample lessons and reflections for future makers to consider as they encounter this movement and community at a very different time, where electronic kits can be ordered off of a Tik Tok shop or Amazon and shipped directly to your front door. Overall, I deeply resonated with a lot of what I saw in these zines, from the incredible stylistic and formatting or design choices, the humorous and often politically motivated maker projects depicted, and the personal and honest reflections about what it means to be a maker, a tinkerer, a designer, an engineer.
