### Recite: Consider what you want to remember, and put that into your own words
- This was another article where I saw a lot of commonality between Hayhurst's experience working with communities as a researcher, and I especially found in her introduction and section on decolonial feminist research a really compelling model for how to right about the tensions that decolonial researchers encounter. 

- What stood out the most about this article was the author reflections on the technologies that are employed specifically for _digital_ participatory action research. In this case, her co-researchers made use of Gopro cameras to capture photos and videos as they were riding their bikes, Which gave her insight to complex issues regarding the risks of using these technologies for research with marginalized communities. For example, She explains how the research participants acknowledged that having video footage of rides was helpful in explaining to local officials and planners some of the risks that these women and girls face while they are biking, but the efficacy of that data for that purpose also highlights an epistemic imbalance in which footage and data are taken as more "authoritative" than the cyclists' reported lived experiences. Hayhurst writes that "Meer and Müller’s (2023) critique of participatory and creative methods Highlight the messy tensions between amplifying a marginalized voices and the risk of reproducing the very systems of coloniality they aim to disrupt. Indeed, their concept of 'epistemic authority' cautions against positioning digital outputs as more valid than lived experiences, emphasizing the need for context-specific reflexivity and equitable redistribution of power within research practices" (p. 13).

### Intervention: What is the intervention that the scholar is making, and where is that intervention being made? In other words, how do you place that intervention relative to existing disciplinary conversations that are taking place?
- Hayhurst Is building on scholarship in participatory action research and decolonial feminist methods for participatory action research to consider the implications of employing digital technologies as part of PAC methods and the various benefits and risks associated with those technologies.

### Argument: What are the core argument(s) being made in the text?
- The concluding section of the Paper includes four key takeaways for Hayhurst based on her experience as a researcher for this study:
    - "First, a feminist ethics of care, embodied research ethic, and 'radical vulnerability' imbued with trust, mutuality, relationality, and reciprocity are essential for emphasizing human dignity, inclusivity, and community in using decolonial feminist DPAR approaches" (p. 16).
    - "Second, researchers trying to balance decolonial commitments with reflexive modes of analysis might expect a messy reality in this 'between space' that operates at the intersection of the self/other nexus to which a decolonial feminist ethic may steer... That is, a decolonial feminist approach provides an opportunity to pry open space for social transformation and justice, and these tenets should ultimately eclipse any perceived or tangible benefits of professional 'benefits' aademics may seek (e.g., producing peer-reviewed publications, which then bolster competitive funding to produce the next grant application, award, etc.)" (p. 16).
    - "Third, despite there being some benefits of using a decolonial feminist DPAR as a Methodology, the complex interplay between digital tools like GoPros and the challenges of addressing SGBV in isolated rural environments impacted by climate change, presented several paradoxes in this study. Though digital technologies provided novel advocacy avenues for documenting bicycle safety, climate change and mobility justice; they also risked reinforcing structural issues such as racism, sexism, and surveillance-imbued safety measures" (p. 16).
    - "Fourth, while the collaboratively developed decolonial feminist DPAR strategies documented unsafe conditions, they also placed an undue burden on the participants to prevent abuse by mapping out dangers and possibly deepened frustrations that these methodologies did not address structural issues, such as repairing bicycles or even being able to ensure new bicycles were readily available and donated" (p. 17).

### Evidence: What evidence is used to support the argument(s)?
- Much of the analytical work of the paper comes from Hayhurst and her co-researchers' reflections as they were collaboratively participating in this research study, sharing their experiences with the technologies they used.
