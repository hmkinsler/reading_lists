### Survey: Skim the text and identify main ideas
- The authors argue that, given the rise of interest in technical and professional communication scholars with decolonial perspectives on research and scholarship, there is a need to interrogate the meaning of "decolonial" as it is used in that scholarly context

- In this particular article, the authors "using a critique of decolonial scholars... reconsider their use of 'decolonial' to describe their experience with urban foraging as a practice that subverts modern Euro-Western foodways" (p. 298)

- They stress the importance of not diluting, misrepresenting, or inappropriately applying the meaning of "decolonial" for research methodologies

- Distinguish between meanings of "anticolonial" versus "decolonial" research

### Relate: Identify connections or links to other readings and scholarship
One of the issues that the authors brought up that stood out to me was there reflections on the affordances and limitations of the mobile technologies that they employed as part of their urban foraging practice. In this case one of the researchers had paid access to an application that used visual object detection models to identify plants. in instances where the two researchers were not already familiar with a specific species more generally, the app was useful in providing a cursory search that would then enable the researchers to seek out additional information and contextualize that information from the perspective of Indigenous knowledge practices. They could look up the Indigenous names for the plants they encountered, for example, or look for local perspectives on the nutritional and medicinal uses of those plants, which revealed tensions between Western epistemologies that often labeled wild plants as "weeds." At the same time, though these apps were employed by the researchers in a generative way, they still rendered visible the violent imposition of Western settler-colonial epistemologies on the design of this technology. Here, I see a connection to Hayhurst's (2025) concern with the risks of employing digital technologies in decolonial research practice, as those technologies are themselves imbued with the dominant oppressive logics that harm the marginalized communities that we are researching from and with.

## Intervention, Argument, Evidence
### Intervention: What is the intervention that the scholar is making, and where is that intervention being made? In other words, how do you place that intervention relative to existing disciplinary conversations that are taking place?
The major goal of this article is for the authors to respond to what they see as a trend in technical and professional communication scholarship of employing the use of the term "decolonial" in contexts where it may obfuscate the action-oriented, material goals of decolonial efforts.

### Argument: What are the core argument(s) being made in the text?
 The primary argument that the authors make is that scholars in technical and professional communication should be careful to ensure that when they describe their methodologies or research practices as decolonial, that that means they are specifically focusing on working toward the goals of rematriation and Indigenous sovereignty. Otherwise, if their focus is on the challenging of colonial norms, ideologies, discourses, and epistemologies, then that scholarship would be more appropriately described as anticolonial.

### Evidence: What evidence is used to support the argument(s)?
Many of the insights from the authors emerged from an experiential narrative analysis that recount their reflections and critical engagement with the meaning of decolonial research as they participated in urban foraging together. They highlight their exploration of Indigenous epistemologies in tension with Western settler-colonial hegemonic perspectives on plants and natural environments, especially in discrepencies between their own perspectives on the plants they encountered, the way they were described in a plant identification app that one of the researchers had paid access for, and a plant identification guide book that they brought along with them.

### How: What methods and theories are used to make the intervention or argument and support it?
The authors draw heavily on Tuck and Yang (2012)'s warning against the use of the term "decolonial" in contexts that are not explicitly geared toward its goals for rematriation, the undoing of settler-colonialism, and Indigenous sovereignty and world-building:
    - "When metaphor invades decolonization, It kills the very possibility of decolonization; It recenters whiteness, it resettles theory, it extends innocence to the settler, it entertains a settler future... Decolonization is not a swappable term for other things we want to do to improve our Societies and schools. Decolonization doesn't have a synonym" (Tuck & Yang, 2012, p. 3)
    - "Within settler colonialism, the most important concern is land/water/air/subterranean earth (land, for shorthand, in this article.) What is most valuable, contested, required. This is both because the settlers make Indigenous land their new home and source of capital, and also because the disruption of Indigenous relationships to land represents a profound epistemic, ontological, cosmological violence. This violence is not temporarily contained in the arrival of the settler but is reasserted each day of occupation" (Tuck & Yang, 2012, p.5)
