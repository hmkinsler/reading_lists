### Relate: Identify connections or links to other readings and scholarship
This article felt like the clearest bridge between the several readings that I've been doing about decolonial approaches to participatory design and participatory action research and Ratto's (2011) critical making framework. She draws on participatory video for her methodology, which is something that Hayhurst notably interrogates, also making use of GoPro cameras in her (2025) study. At one point, Wendy Even makes note of some of the concerns about a lack of alignment between a person's embodied experience and its representation digitally as she's reviewing footage of herself beekeeping, stating that she feels like the camera is retaining too much of a focus on her head movement, making things feel frantic in a way that is distinct from her phenomenological experience while completing her work. This is especially compelling given Hayhurst's caution that the digital technologies employed in participatory action research sometimes pose the risk of contributing to discourses that privilege digital "evidence" of a phenomena over a person's reported lived experience. In this case, not only are there risks with the tendency in scholarly research to prioritize certain forms of data and "evidence" as more legitimate than others, but these technologies are themselves limited in fully representing the phenomena they seek to capture. 

## Intervention, Argument, Evidence
### Intervention: What is the intervention that the scholar is making, and where is that intervention being made? In other words, how do you place that intervention relative to existing disciplinary conversations that are taking place?
Gollihue is responding to the calls of scholars in cultural rhetorics (Powell, 2016) to more concretely focus on the relational nature of making, especially to address an overemphasis in maker culture on technological innovation and production. She argues, "in this way, the cultural logic of the farm---that animal, insect, dirt, and air will inevitably find their way into our own human materialities---pokes holes into the Maker movement's logic that technological human making is unique and separate from the world-building of other things" (p. 24).

### Argument: What are the core argument(s) being made in the text?
Based on her analysis of the videovoice ethnography she and Wendy performed, Gollihue argues that composition instructors should consider the implications of dominant discourses and ideologies in maker culture:

"While critical making provides scholars in our field with the opportunity to explore process, collaboration, and digital literacy as central tenets of composition, we must also recognize the cultural logics we adopt when we uncritically invite the rhetoric of the Maker movement into our classrooms: a privileging of certain ways of knowing, making, and being and marginalization of others. What kinds of composing---what kinds of making--- are lost when we focus entirely on digital forms of invention? By framing the material practices of seemingly non-technological makers like farmers as critical, digital, collaborative, and innovative, we create the opportunity for students from marginal places and identities to see themselves, their communities, and their cultures in the production of academic knowledge" (p. 31).

### Evidence: What evidence is used to support the argument(s)?
Analysis and interviews collected in response to GoPro video footage of the author and her mother working on their farm.

### How: What methods and theories are used to make the intervention or argument and support it?
- Shivers-McNair (2017) on the relationality of making
- Powell's _Makingspace_ (2016)
- Shivers-McNair (2017) and Pink's (2012) video ethnographic methods
