# Notes for Situated knowledges: The science question in feminism and the privilege of partial perspective (Haraway, D., 1998)

## Citation Information
Haraway, D. (1998). Situated knowledges: The science question in feminism and the privilege of partial perspective. *Feminist Studies*, *14*(3), 575-599.

# Reading Notes
## SQR3
### Survey: Skim the text and identify main ideas
- In this article, Haraway Explains the tension that feminist scholars have faced given the historical tendency toward Western, rationalist, empiricist, and positivist perspectives that have priviledged "objectivity" over other forms of knowing in scientific research. She argues that feminist STS scholars have found themselves impacted by a limiting dichotomous perspective on objectivity, on one side of which she feels feminists have so wholly rejected objectivity that they refuse to engage with it and deny their stake in scientific perspectives, and on the other side of which she argues that feminists have sought to so wholly accept objectivity that they are for a reconciliation with it that is a distinctly feminist objectivity.

- She describes the main crux of the issue of objectivity here: "So, I think my problem and 'our' problem is how to have _simultaneously_ an account of radical historical contingency for all knowledge claims and knowing subjects, a critical practice for recognizing our own 'semiotic technologies' for making meanings, _and_ a no-nonsense commitment to faithful accounts of a 'real' world, one that can be partially shared and that is friendly to earthwide projects of finite freedom, adequate material abundance, modest meaning in suffering, and limited happiness" (p. 579)

### Query: Write questions to consider as you read
- How do we make sense of Haraway's call to critically interrogate both of the dichotomous responses to objectivity that she describes, especially in conversation with the non-dual ontology that underlies the posthumanist and new materialist perspective that undergirds much of her work?

### Recite: Consider what you want to remember, and put that into your own words
Much of the argument in this chapter reminds me of Escobar's concern with the modernist onto-epistemological regime characterized by Rationalism, cartesianism, Objectivism, Positivism , and the like. in particular, Haraway's move away from a singular, totalizing epistemological vision of objectivity seems to take resonant with his argument that "..._it is practically impossible to demarcate a single, stable real" (Escobar, 2018, p. 87), a "real" that seems to point at "objectivity." He continues, "to be able to do so, one has to parcel out entire domains of the meshwork as inanimate; this is precisely one of the modern operations par excellence; indeed, moderns imagine the world as an inanimate surface to be _occupied_; for many relational cultures, on the contrary, humans and other beings _inhabit_ a world that is alive."

## Intervention, Argument, Evidence
### Intervention: What is the intervention that the scholar is making, and where is that intervention being made? In other words, how do you place that intervention relative to existing disciplinary conversations that are taking place?
Haroway seeks to respond to what she sees as the limitations of A dichotomy between "radical constructivism" (p. 580) and "feminist critical empiricism," (p. 580) The former of which she fears risks too much of a clinging to issues of semiotics and signification in such a way that objectivity is wholly ignored and thus left open to claim by masculinity, and the latter of which she Argues risks failing to address the very problem posed by objectivity in its efforts to identify and deconstruct sexist bias in science and technology by offering a feminist empiricism in its place, one that may reify the issue of totalizing perspectives in doing so.

### Argument: What are the core argument(s) being made in the text?
in order to address these limitations, Haraway Offers her metaphor for "vision" as a means to understand the situatedness of knowledge. By considering vision, she argues that we are tasked with understanding the distance between our partial subjective positions with the objects that we seek to know, which requires that we consider how we learn to see or what shapes our vision in relation to that we seek to look at. In this way, She offers not an alternative objectivity that still fits within Cartesian dualism, instead suggesting that situatedness itself reveals that "feminist obectivity is about limited location and situated knowledge, not about transcendence and splitting of subject and object" (p. 583).

Vision is thus a process of mediation, through which a partial insight is given into the very thing being mediated. Given the partiality of such knowledge, Haraway suggests that a feminist ontology is one that considers multiple knowledges, enmeshed and entangled in the collectivity of people, semiotic systems, materials, and so on. Given that "self-knowledge requires a semiotic-material system to link meanings and bodies" and "self-identity is a bad visual system" (p. 585), she calls for feminists to recognize the more extensive ontological complexity in which this epistemological work is taking place.

### Evidence: What evidence is used to support the argument(s)?
She largely walks through her metaphor for "vision"

### How: What methods and theories are used to make the intervention or argument and support it?
She maps and responds to scholarship in social constructionism and constructivism, object relations theory, and feminist empiricism, considering their affordances and limitations as she argues in favor of her posthuman and relational perspective on objectivity.
